## How to read this report

**Evidence tiers.** Every substantive claim is tagged so that sourced fact is never confused with argument:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[E]** | **Evidence** — a figure or statement traceable to a named authoritative source, cited inline or in §23. |
| **[I]** | **Inference** — a conclusion I draw by combining two or more pieces of evidence. Defensible, but not itself sourced. |
| **[H]** | **Hypothesis** — a testable proposition offered as a research target, not a finding. |
| **[S]** | **Speculation** — plausible but weakly constrained; flagged so it is never mistaken for either of the above. |

**Figure status.** Casualty and loss figures are additionally marked **CONFIRMED** (settled official figure), **PROVISIONAL** (still being revised at the time of writing), or **ESTIMATE** (modelled, media-derived, or reconciling disagreeing sources). Where sources disagree, the disagreement is reported rather than averaged away. No number in this report was generated by the author.

**A note on timing.** This report was written seven days after the 26 August 2026 Bhote Koshi / Rasuwa transboundary flood catastrophe, which is still an active search-and-recovery operation. Figures for that event are explicitly PROVISIONAL and were rising during the writing of this document. That event is not a footnote here — it changes the strategic conclusion of the entire report, and §1 and §21 explain why.

# 1. Executive Summary

## 1.1 The central finding

**Nepal's dominant disaster-fatality problem is not a forecasting problem. It is a detection-and-dissemination-latency problem.** [I]

That statement is the load-bearing conclusion of this review, and it is contrary to how most "AI for disaster resilience" proposals are written. The evidence for it:

1. **The deadliest recent event carried no meteorological precursor at all.** On 26 August 2026 an ice-and-rock mass detached near Langtang Lirung inside Tibet, fell roughly 1,200 m into the Lhende Khola, formed a temporary debris dam that failed within minutes, and sent a surge down the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli system that raised the river ~9 m in 30 minutes. It was **not raining**. [E — WMO; USGS; Nepalnews] Every rainfall-triggered forecasting model in existence — Google Flood Hub, GLOFAS, LHASA v2, DHM's own bulletins — was structurally blind to it. Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology reportedly learned of the flood roughly 16 minutes *after* it had already crossed the border. [E — reported, *The Print*/Nepali Times] Four of five downstream gauges were destroyed by the flood before their thresholds triggered. [E — reported, Nepali Times]

2. **The one thing that demonstrably saved lives was a phone call plus mass SMS.** A human call at approximately 09:00 triggered 679,295 SMS messages within one minute and evacuated a school of 1,643 students in Bidur. [E — reported, Nepali Times / Kathmandu Post] The technology that saved those children already exists in Nepal. What did not exist was an automated trigger to fire it in the first two minutes rather than the twentieth.

3. **The chronic killer is equally under-warned.** Over 17 July 2018 – 16 July 2024, NDRRMA recorded **859 landslide deaths, 593 fire deaths, 408 lightning deaths, 260 flood deaths, 242 animal-incident deaths, 162 earthquake deaths and 87 windstorm deaths across 32,375 incidents, with NPR 23.60 billion in losses** [E — *Nepal Disaster Report 2024*, CONFIRMED]. Landslides and lightning together — **1,267 deaths in six years, over 200 per year** — have **no national early-warning system of any kind**. [E]

4. **Nepal is already well served by reactive international machinery and poorly served by local anticipatory machinery.** Within roughly 24 hours of 26 August 2026, GDACS auto-issued a Red flood alert, the International Charter activated (#1052), Copernicus EMS activated (EMSR927), Sentinel Asia tasked, Vantor/Maxar and Planet released free very-high-resolution imagery, UNOSAT ran Sentinel-1 flood dashboards, and HOT mobilised volunteer mappers over ~3,000 km². [E] None of that reduced the death toll, because all of it arrived after the water. [I]

**Therefore:** the highest-value AI intervention in Nepal is a system that **detects a mass-movement or surge event within seconds-to-minutes of its initiation, from signals that do not require rainfall and do not require the hazard to be inside Nepal, and converts that detection automatically into a machine-readable CAP alert that fires the SMS/siren infrastructure Nepal already owns.** [I/H]

## 1.2 The nine findings behind that conclusion

| # | Finding | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nepal's mortality is **bimodal**: ~500 deaths/year of chronic attrition (landslide, lightning, fire, flood) plus rare catastrophic tails (8,857 in 2015; 1,000+ in one week in 2026). These require *entirely different* AI interventions and should not be addressed by one system. | [E]+[I] |
| 2 | **Landslides are Nepal's single largest natural-hazard killer in the official record** (859 deaths / 6 years) yet only ~6,000 landslides are mapped nationally, with 40% of the country mapped only once. | [E — Kincey et al. 2025] |
| 3 | **Lightning kills ~68–103 people per year** in Nepal with essentially zero public warning, despite detection networks (GLD360) already observing every strike. This is the cheapest life-saving gap in the country. | [E — NDRRMA; Adhikari et al. 2021] |
| 4 | **Transboundary cryospheric hazard is Nepal's fastest-growing catastrophic risk and its largest structural blind spot.** 25 of the 47 potentially dangerous glacial lakes threatening Nepal's basins sit inside Tibet, China; 11 of 26 recorded GLOFs since 1977 had transboundary impacts; and there is no real-time data-sharing arrangement with China. | [E — ICIMOD/UNDP 2020] |
| 5 | **Earthquake prediction is not scientifically possible and must not be proposed.** Earthquake *early warning* is possible but in Nepal offers only seconds because ruptures are near-source; the higher-value seismic AI work is rapid damage estimation and — importantly — using seismometers as **mass-movement detectors**. | [E — USGS; Geller et al. 1997] +[I] |
| 6 | **Nepali NLP is not ready for operational disaster information extraction, and the failure is concentrated exactly where disasters happen.** No Nepali-language disaster corpus exists anywhere. The one Nepali NER set with an Event class scores F1 0.44 on it. Romanized Nepali shows the largest native→romanized LLM accuracy drop of any language yet measured (>15, up to 24 points). Nepali ASR WER remains ~24% even fine-tuned. | [E] |
| 7 | **Optical satellite imagery is unusable over much of Nepal in exactly the months disasters occur** — parts of the northern mountains are expected to have *no* cloud-free days between June and September — and SAR's layover/shadow geometry disables a large share of steep terrain. Any Nepal EO plan that assumes Sentinel-2 will be available during the monsoon is invalid. | [E] |
| 8 | **Nepal's only institutionally "AI-ready" decision point already exists**: the OCHA/WFP/NRCS anticipatory-action frameworks run on hard numeric dual-condition triggers (GLOFAS probability + DHM bulletin, or gauge danger level at Chisapani 10.5 m / Chatara 7.0 m) and release money without a fresh human decision. A new model only has to beat an already-accepted trigger, not invent institutional trust. | [E — OCHA Nepal AA Framework] |
| 9 | **Nepal's disaster data has one dominant unknown**: whether the BIPAD portal exposes a usable API. This single unresolved question determines the feasibility of half the projects proposed here and should be answered by a phone call to NDRRMA in week one, not assumed. | [E/I] |

## 1.3 The recommended portfolio, in one table

Five projects are proposed in §12 and scored in §13. Scores are out of 10; "Overall" is the weighted composite defined in §13.1.

| Rank | Project | What it is | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **GHADI** — Sub-hourly cascading-flood **detection** and auto-dissemination for transboundary Himalayan rivers | Multimodal detection (seismic mass-movement signatures + gauge rate-of-rise anomaly + satellite lake monitoring) → automatic CAP alert. Detection, not prediction. | **8.5** |
| **2** | **NEPAL-CRISIS-IE** — Nepali / Romanized / code-mixed disaster information-extraction corpus, model and missing-person record-linkage | The first Nepali crisis-NLP dataset in existence, with an operational application to the 4,600-person missing register created by the 2026 flood. | **8.0** |
| **3** | **HIMAL-SLIDE** — Nepal-calibrated landslide nowcasting + foundation-model national landslide inventory | Downscale and recalibrate LHASA v2 for Nepal; fine-tune a geospatial foundation model to detect the small (<50 m) landslides Sentinel-2 misses. | **7.6** |
| **4** | **SADAK** — Post-disaster road accessibility and rescue-priority graph | CV road-blockage detection + OSM/HOT road graph + GNN accessibility + NLP from situation reports. Addresses the failure that recurs in 2015, 2023, 2024 and 2026. | **7.1** |
| **5** | **BIJULI** — Lightning nowcasting and community alerting | 30–60 minute convective-cell lightning alerts to high-fatality districts. Lowest novelty, highest deaths-averted-per-dollar. | **6.8** |

**Start GHADI and NEPAL-CRISIS-IE in parallel.** GHADI is the high-impact, high-risk system project; NEPAL-CRISIS-IE is the low-risk project that will produce a publishable artefact even if GHADI's field validation fails, because dataset papers publish. That pairing is the correct risk posture for a small team with limited funding and a 12-month horizon. Full reasoning in §21 and the Decision Memo.

## 1.4 What this report deliberately does *not* recommend

- **Do not build an earthquake prediction system.** It is not possible. [E]
- **Do not build another rainfall-driven flood forecasting model for Nepal's major gauged rivers.** GLOFAS, Google Flood Hub and DHM's own CBEWS already occupy that space; marginal AI gains there are small and the deaths are elsewhere. [I]
- **Do not build a general-purpose "disaster AI platform."** Nepal has BIPAD. A parallel platform will be orphaned. Build modules that emit into BIPAD and CAP. [I]
- **Do not assume optical imagery during the monsoon.** [E]
- **Do not deploy an off-the-shelf multimodal LLM on Nepali disaster imagery or Nepali text without a measured hallucination audit.** GeoChat's own authors report that general VLMs fabricate content on remote-sensing scenes. [E]

# 2. Nepal Disaster Landscape

## 2.1 The official mortality baseline

The most internally consistent national dataset is NDRRMA's six-year incident record published in the *Nepal Disaster Report 2024*, covering **17 July 2018 – 16 July 2024** [E, CONFIRMED]:

| Hazard | Incidents | Deaths | Deaths/yr (mean) | Recorded economic loss (NPR) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Fire (predominantly structural/household) | 19,593 | 593 | ~99 | 17.63 bn |
| **Landslide** | 2,743 | **859** | **~143** | 796.9 m |
| Lightning | 1,699 | 408 | ~68 | — |
| Flood | 927 | 260 | ~43 | — |
| Animal incidents | 1,743 | 242 | ~40 | — |
| Windstorm | 1,013 | 87 | ~15 | 433.5 m |
| Earthquake | 163 | 162 | ~27 | 2.48 bn |
| **All hazards (total)** | **32,375** | **2,996** | **~500** | **23.60 bn** |
| *(Road accidents, reported separately)* | *120,626* | *15,169* | *~2,528* | — |

Four observations matter for research prioritisation:

- **[I] Landslides dominate.** They kill more than floods, earthquakes and windstorms combined in this window, and they have no early-warning system.
- **[I] Lightning outranks floods.** A hazard with sub-second physics and no warning system kills ~50% more Nepalis per year than riverine flooding does — and lightning warning is technologically trivial compared with flood forecasting.
- **[I] The earthquake figure is misleading and must not be used for prioritisation.** 162 deaths in six years reflects a window containing only the moderate 2023 Jajarkot event. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake killed 8,857 people in one morning [E — Government of Nepal, CONFIRMED]. Earthquake risk is a *tail* risk and must be evaluated on expected-loss, not on frequency-weighted mortality.
- **[E/I] Slow-onset hazards are absent from the table entirely.** Drought and heat have no mortality row, which reflects a reporting-system design (incident-based, sudden-onset) rather than an absence of impact. This is itself a data gap.

## 2.2 Hazard-by-hazard evidence

### Earthquakes
Nepal sits on the Main Himalayan Thrust. **2015 Gorkha (Mw 7.8, 25 April): 8,857 deaths in Nepal, 21,952 injured, ~700,000+ buildings destroyed, ~USD 10 bn damage [E, CONFIRMED / ESTIMATE for damage].** Mortality was extraordinarily unevenly distributed — 542.4 deaths per 100,000 in mountain districts versus 0.96 per 100,000 in the Terai [E — peer-reviewed retrospective mortality analysis]. **2023 Jajarkot/Rukum West (3 Nov, Mw 5.7 USGS / ML 6.4 NEMRC): 154 deaths (101 Jajarkot, 53 Rukum West), 356 injured, 26,657 houses fully and 35,455 partially damaged, NPR 2.47 bn loss [E — Nepal Disaster Report 2024, CONFIRMED].** Nearly half the dead were children; the event struck at 23:47 with people asleep in stone-and-mud housing [E].

Monitoring: Nepal operates ~42 seismic stations, 50 GPS stations and 36 accelerometers via DMG/NEMRC [E]; the network is FDSN-registered as "NK" (DOI 10.7914/SN/NK) and waveforms are reachable through standard FDSN/IRIS clients [E] — a genuinely usable open data asset. There is **no operational earthquake early-warning system**; a Duke–Tribhuvan pilot is in R&D for 2025–26 [E].

**Prediction status [E]:** USGS states plainly that earthquake prediction is not possible; Geller et al. (1997, *Science*) remains the standard statement that no precursor method has demonstrated skill beyond chance. Nothing in the ML literature reviewed in §5 contradicts this. Every credible "AI for earthquakes" result concerns *post-rupture detection* or *post-shaking damage estimation*.

### Monsoon floods and flash floods
927 recorded flood incidents and 260 deaths in six years [E], but single events dominate: **September 2024 (26–28 Sept) central Nepal floods and landslides: 250 deaths (Government of Nepal final figure, 16 Oct 2024), 18 missing, 4,667 houses destroyed, 23 highways affected with 3 fully severing access to Kathmandu, NPR 2.5 bn road/bridge and NPR 4.35 bn energy-infrastructure damage; 81,000+ people affected [E, CONFIRMED].** NDRRMA's own preliminary loss assessment put total losses at NPR 46.68 bn with roads alone NPR 27.98 bn [E].

Nepal's deadliest recorded single flood remains the **1993 Kulekhani cloudburst flood, cited at 1,336 deaths on the government's own DRR Portal** [E, CONFIRMED-per-official-source but not independently cross-validated against DesInventar/EM-DAT in this review — flagged].

Forecast capability: DHM operates a flood EWS on selected basins (Bagmati, Karnali, West Rapti) with 28 telemetric hydrological and 88 meteorological stations, and required lead times spanning **~15 minutes (Bagmati at Sundarijal) to ~22 hours (Karnali at Chisapani)** — run by a section of roughly five professional staff and six technicians [E — DHM Flood EWS SOP]. Station-count figures are inconsistent across sources (a 2026 report cites ~200 hydrological and 360 automatic weather stations feeding BIPAD) [E, conflicting].

### Landslides
**859 deaths / 2,743 incidents in six years — Nepal's largest natural-hazard killer in the official record** [E]. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake alone triggered 4,312 rapidly-mapped landslides [E — NASA/Kargel et al.]; more exhaustive inventories report higher counts. A systematic review of 117 Nepal landslide inventories (2010–2021) found a national areal density of only **0.05 landslides/km², extrapolating to ~6,000 mapped landslides nationally, with 40% of the country mapped only once** and mapping strongly biased toward the 2015 epicentral zone and highway corridors [E — Kincey et al., *Natural Hazards* 2025].

There is **no national landslide early-warning system**. Deforestation 5–7 years prior raises landslide probability by ~16% in Far-Western Nepal [E — Muñoz-Torrero Manchado et al. 2022, *CATENA*], which means static land-cover layers used in most susceptibility maps are structurally inadequate.

### GLOFs and transboundary cryospheric cascade
The authoritative inventory is ICIMOD/UNDP (2020), based on 2015 Landsat imagery [E]:

| | Count |
|---|---:|
| Glacial lakes mapped, Koshi + Gandaki + Karnali basins | **3,624** |
| — of which in Nepal | **2,070** |
| — of which in TAR, China | 1,509 |
| — of which in India | 45 |
| **Potentially dangerous glacial lakes (PDGLs), total** | **47** |
| — in Nepal | 21 |
| — **in TAR, China** | **25** |
| — in India | 1 |
| PDGLs by basin | Koshi 42, Gandaki 3, Karnali 2 |
| Recorded GLOFs affecting Nepal since 1977 | **26** |
| — of which had transboundary impacts | **11** |

**[I] This table is the single most important risk finding in the report.** A majority of the lakes that can kill Nepalis are not in Nepal. Nepal's own DRR Portal still displays a superseded figure of 2,315 lakes / 15 dangerous [E, conflicting official sources] — a data-governance problem in its own right.

Nepal built one of the world's earliest GLOF EWS at Tsho Rolpa (1998, solar sirens) and later at Imja [E]; the 1990s Tsho Rolpa system was later stripped during the insurgency [E], a durability lesson. The **August 2024 Thame GLOF** (Thyanbo lake, Solukhumbu) destroyed 14 properties including a school and health post with **zero deaths** because residents self-evacuated on sight [E] — and critically, the lake had been below the size threshold used by standard hazard screening, so it was never on any dangerous-lake list [E — NHESS 2026]. **[I] Size-thresholded PDGL inventories are a demonstrated failure mode.**

### Lightning
Peer-reviewed analysis using MoHA/DRR Portal fatality data and the GLD360 detection network found **an average of 103 deaths and 273 injuries per year over 2011–2020 (930 deaths, 2,454 injuries total), a fatality rate of 3.8 per million per year, highest in Okhaldhunga and Solukhumbu** [E — Adhikari et al. 2021]. NDRRMA's overlapping 2018–2024 window records 408 deaths [E]. The two are consistent in order of magnitude. The authors state explicitly that there is **no early-warning or forecasting system** for lightning in Nepal [E].

### Wildfire
ICIMOD/SERVIR-HKH's Forest Fire Detection and Monitoring System has run since 2012, ingesting MODIS/VIIRS hotspots three times daily with district/protected-area enrichment and email alerting to the Department of Forests, plus a two-day fire-risk outlook added in 2023 [E]. Incident counts: **4,528 fires in 2021** (a record), 4,231 in 2016, and a 76.5% surge in Jan–Apr 2023 (1,816 incidents) versus all of 2022 [E]. Roughly 41,000 satellite hotspots were detected between 1 January and 7 April 2021 [E — NASA Earth Observatory]. **[I] Wildfire is the one hazard where Nepal already has a working, government-integrated, low-latency EO pipeline — and it should be studied as the template for every other hazard rather than rebuilt.**

### Extreme rainfall, drought and heat
DHM recorded **624.0 mm in 24 hours at Kanchanpur on 8 July 2024 — the highest ever recorded in Nepal, over a 78-year record** [E]. Attribution work published in 2026 finds the September 2024 central-Nepal rainfall was ~10% more intense and ~70% more likely in today's ~1.3 °C-warmer climate, and that heat extremes are up to 3× more likely and up to 200% more intense in the Middle Mountains [E — *Frontiers in Climate* 2026]. The same study finds floods and landslides together caused **~70% of Nepal's disaster fatalities over the past three decades** [E]. 2023 was Nepal's second-warmest year since 1962 [E — DHM]. No heat-health warning system exists [E].

### Avalanche and snowstorm
Not systematically captured in NDRRMA's civilian incident tables. Reference events: **October 2014 Annapurna/Thorong La snowstorm — at least 43 dead, up to 50 missing, ~175 injured, 407 rescued** [E]; **April 2014 Everest icefall — 16 Nepali guides killed** [E]; **November 2025 Yalung Ri/Panbari storms — at least 9 dead, 5 presumed dead** [E]. No trekking-route weather-alert system exists [E].

### Cascading and compound events
Nepal's fatal-event record is increasingly chained rather than single-hazard [I]: earthquake → landslide (2015); rainfall → debris flow → landslide dam → outburst flood (Melamchi 2021; Jure 2014); ice/rock avalanche → temporary dam → outburst surge across an international border (Rasuwa 2025 and 2026). WMO explicitly framed the August 2026 event as a cryosphere cascading-hazard case requiring cross-border data exchange [E].

## 2.4 Answers to the six framing questions

**1. Which hazards cause the greatest mortality?**
Chronic: **landslides** (859/6yr), then fire (593, mostly structural), lightning (408), floods (260) [E]. Tail: **earthquakes** (8,857 in a single 2015 morning) and now **transboundary cryospheric cascade** (1,000+ dead and 4,600+ unaccounted for in a single 2026 morning, PROVISIONAL) [E].

**2. Greatest displacement and infrastructure damage?**
Floods, by a wide margin. The 2017 Terai floods affected ~1.7 million people across 35 districts, destroyed 43,400 homes and damaged 191,700 [E]. September 2024 caused NPR 46.68 bn in losses, NPR 27.98 bn of it roads [E]. The August 2026 event destroyed 35 motorable and 45 suspension bridges, ~40 km of road, and took 431 MW of hydropower offline, with reconstruction estimated at USD 4–5 bn [E, ESTIMATE].

**3. Which hazards are becoming harder because of climate change?**
GLOF and cryospheric cascade (unambiguously — new lakes forming, ice-rock slope stability declining) [E/I]; extreme rainfall (attributed: +10% intensity, +70% likelihood for the Sept 2024 event) [E]; heat (up to 3× more likely, +200% intensity in the Middle Mountains) [E]; and rainfall-triggered landslides as a second-order consequence of both [I].

**4. Largest predictive/early-warning opportunity for AI?**
Ranked by (physical feasibility × current absence of warning): **(i) cascading-flood surge detection**, **(ii) landslide nowcasting**, **(iii) lightning nowcasting**, **(iv) flash-flood nowcasting in ungauged hill catchments**. [I]

**5. Biggest information gaps?**
(i) No real-time upstream data from Tibet; (ii) no national landslide inventory (only ~6,000 mapped, 40% of country mapped once); (iii) no post-2013 open structured national loss database (DesInventar Nepal stops at 2013); (iv) no Nepali-language disaster text corpus of any kind; (v) no building-level damage labels for any Nepal event; (vi) no road-blockage/passability dataset; (vii) sparse sub-daily rainfall gauges in hill catchments, with CHIRPS/IMERG/ERA5 all documented as biased in steep Himalayan terrain. [E]

**6. Where can casualties be reduced most effectively?**
**Not at prediction. At detection and at the last mile.** [I] The evidence: Thame 2024 (self-evacuation on sight → zero deaths); Bidur 2026 (one phone call → 1,643 children evacuated); Melamchi 2021 (informal upstream phone relay reduced casualties, and ICIMOD noted the HIWAT model *could* have forecast it two days ahead but was never operationalised as a warning); October 2025 (warnings issued, roads pre-closed, teams pre-positioned — widely reported as a markedly better outcome than 2024). Every one of these is a dissemination story, not a modelling story.

### 3.1.1 — 26 August 2026 · Bhote Koshi / Trishuli transboundary cascade · Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading

**The most consequential event for this report, and still unresolved at the time of writing.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Date/time | 26 Aug 2026, ~08:37–08:40 NPT |
| Hazard | Ice-and-rock avalanche → temporary debris dam → dam-break surge (cryospheric cascade). Not a classical moraine-dammed GLOF. |
| Origin | Slope near Langtang Lirung, **inside Tibet (TAR, China)**; ~600 m-wide mass, ~1,200 m fall into the Lhende Khola [E] |
| Mechanism | Debris dam failed within ~3 minutes; Trishuli rose **~9 m in 30 minutes** [E — WMO] |
| Meteorology | **It was not raining** [E] |
| Seismic signature | USGS confirmed the signal initially interpreted as an M4.4 earthquake was **landslide-generated, not tectonic** [E] |
| Deaths | **PROVISIONAL and rising.** 538 (28 Aug, UN News) → 734–768 (30 Aug) → 1,003–1,058 remains recovered (1 Sept, Nepal Police via Nepalnews). Reporting within a single outlet on 1 Sept ranged 1,003 / 1,058 / 1,508. **Treat as "in excess of 1,000, not settled."** |
| Composition of recovered remains (1 Sept, 14:00) | 361 men, 232 women, 35 female children, 30 male children, 390 partial remains [E, PROVISIONAL] |
| Missing / unaccounted | **4,606**, including 2,683 Nepali citizens and 583 foreign nationals from 38 countries [E, PROVISIONAL — note the stated sub-totals do not sum to the headline figure; the residual is unattributed in the source] |
| Injured / rescued | 4,530 (1 Sept); earlier tallies of 8,000–11,884 rescued [E, conflicting] |
| Holding centres by district | Nuwakot 2,466; Rasuwa 398; Kathmandu 329; Dhading 260 [E] |
| Infrastructure | 6–9 hydropower plants destroyed (431 MW off-grid); 35 motorable + 45 suspension bridges destroyed; ~40 km road destroyed; Pasang Lhamu Highway and Prithvi Highway severed; Rasuwagadhi customs post and ~300 vehicles destroyed; 18 schools destroyed, 20 damaged [E] |
| Response | 21,000+ security personnel; 7,912 Nepal Police (65 affected, 25 out of contact); ~2,000 Army + 7 Army helicopters supplemented by **8 privately chartered tourism helicopters**; 411+ flights by 1 Sept; Chinese and Indian specialists; USD 42 m+ pledged; Nepal's first claim to the UN Loss and Damage Fund [E] |

**What information was missing:** real-time monitoring of the source slope inside Tibet; any Nepal–China transboundary hazard data-sharing protocol (a gap the Stimson Center had documented *before* this event, following the July 2025 precursor at the same location); gauge instrumentation that measures **rate of rise** rather than absolute stage; an automated trigger between a detected anomaly and the SMS infrastructure; and a pre-event registry of trekkers and foreign nationals in the Langtang–Rasuwagadhi corridor, which is why 583 foreign nationals from 38 countries remained unaccounted for weeks later. [E/I]

**What AI could plausibly have done [H]:** Not predicted the collapse. But a classifier running on continuous waveform data from the NK seismic network — and on Chinese/Indian regional stations available through FDSN — could plausibly have flagged an anomalous, long-duration, emergent, non-tectonic seismic signal within **60–180 seconds** of the mass movement, and a rate-of-rise anomaly detector on the surviving upstream gauge could have corroborated it within another few minutes. Against a surge that took roughly 20–40 minutes to reach the first Nepali settlements, that is a materially actionable window. This is the entire thesis of Project GHADI (§12.1).

### 3.1.2 — 8 July 2025 · Bhote Koshi / Rasuwagadhi supraglacial lake drainage (the ignored precursor)

Supraglacial lake (~5,150 m) drained, producing an ice/debris avalanche into the river. **9 confirmed dead, 19 missing** [E, PROVISIONAL at the time]. Destroyed the Miteri/Friendship Bridge — the main Nepal–China trade crossing — and removed 211 MW from the national grid [E]. **No transboundary lake-monitoring or data-sharing arrangement existed for this exact lake**, a gap explicitly flagged afterwards by Stimson Center analysis [E]. **[I] The same corridor, the same failure mode, and the same missing capability, thirteen months before it killed a thousand people. This is the strongest possible argument that the gap is institutional and technical, not epistemic — Nepal was told.**

### 3.1.3 — 26–28 September 2024 · Kathmandu Valley and central Nepal floods and landslides

**250 deaths (Government of Nepal final, 16 Oct 2024: 76 female, 114 male, 60 children), 18 missing** [E, CONFIRMED]; the toll progressed 104 → 193 → 217 → 236 → 246 → 250 across successive sitreps [E]. 4,667 houses destroyed, 5,310 partially damaged; 23 highways affected with 3 fully blocking access to Kathmandu; 190+ schools, 62 health facilities, 550 water systems damaged; 81,000+ affected; 71 municipalities across 20 districts declared disaster zones; 30,000+ security personnel deployed [E]. Government described it as the heaviest rainfall recorded for that period [E].

**Causes of death:** a large share were people in vehicles swept from, or trapped on, flooded and landslide-blocked highways at night on the Narayangadh–Mugling corridor; NPR reporting documents people asleep in vehicles trapped by a landslide-blocked road [E]. **[I] This is a road-network information failure, not a rainfall-forecast failure — general awareness that record rain was falling existed; location-specific, real-time road-closure alerting to people already in transit did not.** Directly motivates Project SADAK (§12.4).

---

### 3.1.4 — 16 August 2024 · Thame GLOF, Solukhumbu

Thyanbo glacial lake (~0.05 km²) drained. **Zero deaths.** 14 properties destroyed including a school, a health post, 5 hotels and 7 homes; the village evacuated in time on visual observation [E]. The lake was **below the size threshold used by standard PDGL screening and was therefore not on any dangerous-lake list**; ICIMOD noted its rapid expansion and contraction were not being tracked in real time and the event had to be reconstructed after the fact from satellite imagery [E — NHESS 2026].

**[I] Two lessons, both important. First: sight-based evacuation works — the last mile is not hopeless. Second: threshold-based hazard inventories systematically miss the small lakes, and "monitor all lakes above a minimal size" is the correct design rule, not "monitor the 21 PDGLs."**

### 3.1.5 — 3 November 2023 · Jajarkot / Rukum West earthquake

**154 deaths (101 Jajarkot, 53 Rukum West), 356 injured, 26,657 houses fully damaged, 35,455 partially, NPR 2.47 bn loss** [E — Nepal Disaster Report 2024, CONFIRMED; media consensus at the time reported 153]. ~67,000 families displaced in Jajarkot; 91 schools destroyed and 122 damaged; 20+ health facilities destroyed including the district hospital [E]. Struck at 23:47 with people asleep in stone/mud/log housing that pancaked; nearly half the dead were children [E].

**Missing information:** no rapid structural-damage triage capability — army and police went door to door; no current building-vulnerability inventory for far-western hill districts [E/I].

### 3.1.6 — 25 April 2015 · Gorkha earthquake (Mw 7.8) and the Langtang avalanche

**8,857 deaths in Nepal (8,962 including cross-border), 21,952 injured, ~2.6 million internal displacements** [E, CONFIRMED]. 30+ UNESCO monuments collapsed. The 12 May Mw 7.3 aftershock added 153+ deaths [E]. The **Langtang** earthquake-triggered ice/rock avalanche (14.4 million m³) killed or left missing **more than 350 people**, with most deaths caused by the *air blast* rather than the ice itself, amplified by anomalous snowfall that winter increasing mobility [E — *Communications Earth & Environment* 2024]. The Everest icefall avalanche the same day killed ~19 [E].

**Missing information:** no ward-level building-vulnerability inventory to triage rescue; **no live landslide-blockage map of the road network**, which delayed relief convoys to Gorkha, Sindhupalchok and Dolakha by days [E/I]. Cloud cover compounded it: even outside the monsoon, mountainous parts of the affected region waited an average of **~16.8 days for a cloud-free image** versus ~1–2 days in Kathmandu Valley [E].

### 3.1.7 — 15 June 2021 · Melamchi flood, Sindhupalchok

Cascading chain: 200+ mm rain over six days, rapid snowmelt, a glacial-lake-related outburst in the Pemdang Khola headwaters, breach of an old landslide dam at Bremthang, and a fresh 670,000 m³ landslide at Melamchigaon. **5 dead, 20 missing, 6 injured; 525 families displaced; 337 houses destroyed; 13 suspension and 7 motorable bridges damaged; the newly built Melamchi Drinking Water Project (supplying Kathmandu) severely damaged; >NPR 1 bn loss** [E]. Informal upstream-to-downstream phone relay gave some communities notice and reduced casualties [E]. **ICIMOD noted the HIWAT hydrological model could in principle have forecast the flood two days ahead — but it was never operationalised as a public warning** [E]. **[I] A model that exists but is not wired to a siren saves nobody. This is the report's recurring theme.**

---

### 3.1.8 — 2 August 2014 · Jure / Sunkoshi landslide and landslide dam

Monsoon-triggered slope failure buried Jure village and dammed the Sunkoshi. Reported tolls climbed from 33 (5 Aug) to a widely cited **~156 dead and missing combined** [E, ESTIMATE — sources vary on whether this counts confirmed dead or dead-plus-missing]. The 5.5 million m³ debris dam created a lake 47 m deep, blocked the Araniko Highway (the main Nepal–China trade route) and destroyed 5 km of road. **The Nepal Army took 45 days to excavate a controlled drainage channel by hand and dynamite** while tens of thousands downstream lived under dam-break risk with no monitoring or warning system [E].

### 3.1.9 — August–September 2017 Terai floods

**160 deaths (final), 16 missing, 44 injured; 1.7 million people affected across 35 districts; 461,000 people (91,400 families) displaced at peak; 43,400 homes destroyed and 191,700 partially damaged** [E, CONFIRMED]. Largest affected-population flood in Nepal's modern record. No integrated cross-border flood forecast with India; embankment-breach risk was not tracked in real time [E].

## 3.2 Recurring failure patterns

Seven patterns recur across every event above [I]:

1. **A signal existed and never reached the exposed population through a formal channel.** Melamchi 2021 (a model that could have run two days early), Sept 2024 (record-rainfall awareness without road-specific alerting), Rasuwa 2025 and 2026 (an upstream hazard known to analysts). The failure is almost never "nobody could have known."
2. **Transboundary blindness is the single most consequential structural gap.** 2016 Gongbatongsha, 2025 Rasuwagadhi, 2026 Bhote Koshi — three events, one corridor, one missing agreement.
3. **Casualty figures are chronically volatile.** Sept 2024 ran 104→250; Lidi 18→37; the 2020 season differs 3× between government and modelled figures; the August 2026 toll was revised upward daily. **No single authoritative real-time casualty registry reconciles police, army, hospital and community-reported deaths.** [I] This is itself an addressable information-systems problem, and it is the operational core of Project NEPAL-CRISIS-IE.
4. **Hazard mapping lags known risk.** Sindhupalchok (Jure 2014, Lidi 2020) and the Langtang/Rasuwa corridor (2015, 2025, 2026) were each struck repeatedly without a persistent inventory informing settlement or infrastructure siting.
5. **Tourists and foreign nationals are a structural blind spot.** 2014 Annapurna and 2026 Bhote Koshi (583 foreign nationals, 38 countries) both show Nepal has no real-time count of who is in a remote corridor.
6. **Road and bridge failure converts every disaster into a second-order access crisis** — 2015, 2023, 2024, 2026 alike — and in no event was a live road-passability map used operationally.
7. **When warnings are issued and acted on, outcomes visibly improve** (Thame 2024: zero deaths; Bidur 2026: 1,643 children; Oct 2025: pre-positioned response). **[I] The binding constraint is dissemination capacity and institutional will, not physical impossibility.**

# 4. Casualty Reduction Opportunities

## 4.1 The disaster lifecycle, and where AI actually helps

For each hazard the lifecycle is **Before → Early Warning → During → Search & Rescue → Relief → Recovery**. The table below states, per hazard and stage, the intervention, the required data, the model class, deployment difficulty (1–10, 10 = hardest), latency achievable, reliability expectation, and Nepal feasibility (1–10, 10 = readily feasible).

### 4.1.1 Landslide

| Stage | Intervention | Data | Model | Deploy diff. | Latency | Reliability | Nepal feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---:|
| Before | National susceptibility + **runout** mapping at ward level | DEM (FABDEM/GLO-30), lithology, land cover, landslide inventory, rainfall climatology | RF/XGBoost + physically-based runout | 4 | Static, annual refresh | AUC ~0.94 reported for co-seismic Nepal case [E] | 8 |
| Before | Exposure: population and buildings in runout zones | WorldPop, VIDA Google-Microsoft-OSM buildings, census wards | Zonal statistics + graph | 3 | Static | High | 9 |
| Early warning | **Nepal-recalibrated rainfall-threshold nowcast** | IMERG + DHM gauges + SMAP soil moisture + susceptibility | XGBoost (LHASA v2 class), Nepal-tuned | 6 | 3–24 h | LHASA v2 multitemporal TPR only **17–34%** globally [E] — Nepal tuning is the research | 6 |
| During | Landslide detection and dating | Sentinel-1 backscatter time series (all-weather), PlanetScope | Change detection + CNN/foundation-model segmentation | 5 | 12–72 h | Small (<50 m) slides are near/below Sentinel-2's floor [E] | 6 |
| SAR | Access and blockage on the road graph | OSM/HOT roads, VHR/UAV, situation reports | GNN accessibility + CV + NLP | 6 | 6–24 h | Moderate | 7 |
| Relief | Prioritisation by exposed-and-isolated population | Above + shelter/health facility layers | Multi-criteria + graph | 4 | Hours | Moderate | 8 |
| Recovery | Reactivation monitoring of the same scars | InSAR (Sentinel-1, NISAR L-band) | PS-InSAR + ML | 8 | Weeks | Coherence loss in vegetated steep terrain is severe [E] | 4 |

**Casualty-reduction mechanism [H]:** landslide deaths in Nepal are overwhelmingly people buried in dwellings, often at night, in known-unstable settlements. The realistic mechanism is *not* minute-scale warning; it is **(a) pre-monsoon relocation/reinforcement decisions driven by ward-level susceptibility+runout+exposure, and (b) 6–24 hour "evacuate to the community building tonight" advisories on high-rainfall nights**. That second mechanism is the one Nepal has never had.

**Casualty-reduction mechanism [H]:** on 26 August 2026 the surge took an estimated 20–40 minutes to reach the first settlements. A detection at T+2 minutes with automatic dissemination at T+3 minutes yields roughly 17–37 minutes of warning. ### 4.1.4 Earthquake

| Stage | Intervention | Feasibility verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Prediction | — | **Not scientifically possible. Do not propose it.** [E] |
| Early warning (EEW) | Seconds-scale P-wave alerting | Physically possible but **near-source geometry limits Kathmandu to single-digit-to-low-tens of seconds**; high capex; Nepal has no operational system, only a 2025–26 Duke/TU pilot [E]. **Not recommended as a first project.** |
| Detection | Faster, more complete catalogues via ML phase-picking | Feasible today on free FDSN data; EQTransformer-class models detect ~2.4× more events than manual catalogues in test regions [E], though generalisation to sparse networks is untested [E]. **High value as infrastructure, and it is the same pipeline GHADI needs.** |

| Damage assessment | Building-level damage grade from VHR/UAV | Feasible; xBD does **not** contain the 2015 Nepal earthquake [E], and top xView2 models degrade on out-of-distribution, context-independent damage judgements [E — xFBD]. Nepal-specific labels are required |
| SAR/relief | Road access, shelter allocation | See §4.1.1/SADAK |

**[I] The honest earthquake conclusion:** the largest avoidable earthquake mortality in Nepal is addressed by building codes and retrofitting, not by AI. AI's defensible contributions are (i) faster damage triage, (ii) better exposure/vulnerability data, and (iii) using the seismic network for *non-earthquake* hazard detection.

### 4.1.5 Lightning

Nowcasting convective cells 30–60 minutes ahead from geostationary IR, radar proxies and lightning-detection-network history is an established ML problem. Nepal has ~68–103 deaths/yr and **zero** warning [E]. Deployment path: district-level alerts via FM radio (the dominant rural channel) and SMS, plus school/agricultural-worker targeting since fatalities concentrate among people working outdoors. **Deployment difficulty 4; Nepal feasibility 8; novelty low.** [I]

### 4.1.6 Wildfire

Already solved operationally in Nepal by SERVIR-HKH/ICIMOD + Department of Forests, at near-real-time latency with automated alerting [E]. **The correct research action is to study this pipeline as the institutional template, not to rebuild it.** [I] Residual research value: fire-spread modelling and burn-severity mapping (a Himalayan Prithvi fine-tune already exists as a proof of concept [E]).

# 5. Literature Review

Coverage emphasises 2020–2026 with foundational older work where necessary. Every entry below was verified against a publisher page, arXiv, Crossref or Semantic Scholar during this review; items that could not be verified were dropped rather than cited. Full references in §23.

## 5.1 Flood forecasting with machine learning

**The two systems that define the field.** Nevo et al. (2022, *HESS*) describe Google's operational flood system: LSTM stage forecasting plus a linear-regression ensemble, with inundation maps produced by a learned "manifold model" replacing hydraulic simulation. Deployed over India and Bangladesh in 2021 (~470,000 km²), median Persistence-NSE 0.69 for stage and median inundation F1 69–76%, 8–48 h lead. **Critically, the authors state the system requires 6–7 years of gauge history and is built for gauged, slow-responding rivers, and explicitly cannot handle ungauged basins or flash floods in catchments below ~1,000 km²** [E]. **[I] That exclusion is a precise description of Nepal's hill hydrology.**

Nearing et al. (2024, *Nature*) closes part of that gap: an encoder–decoder LSTM trained on 152,259 basin-years across 5,680 watersheds, taking no streamflow as input, predicting 1–10-year return-period events up to 5 days ahead in **ungauged** basins and beating GLOFAS nowcasts in most regions, improving on 2-year events at 70% of gauges [E]. Performance degrades in large and arid basins, and **the paper reports no HKH-specific validation** [E] — a live evidence gap for Nepal.

Foundationally, Kratzert et al. (2019, *WRR* and *HESS*) showed a single LSTM trained across hundreds of CAMELS basins outperforms basin-calibrated conceptual models and the US National Water Model on withheld basins [E]. **[I] The theoretical basis for regional deep learning in ungauged Nepali catchments is sound; the empirical basis is absent.**

**Nowcasting and AI weather models.** DGMR (Ravuri et al. 2021, *Nature*) and NowcastNet (Zhang et al. 2023, *Nature*) advanced 0–3 h radar nowcasting; independent evaluation on 30 US extreme events found NowcastNet median CSI 0.30 vs HRRR 0.04 for >16 mm/h, but 65–90% of residual error is *pattern* error and skill collapses beyond 1–2 h [E — Das et al. 2024]. Pangu-Weather (Bi et al. 2023, *Nature*) beats IFS on RMSE by ~10% at ~10,000× the speed but **excludes precipitation and underestimates extremes** by its own authors' account [E].

**The single most important paper for Nepal in this strand** is Gupta, Sheshadri & Suri (2025, arXiv:2509.01879), which evaluated seven AI weather models (FourCastNet, Pangu, GraphCast, Aurora, GenCast, AIFS) over the **South Asian monsoon** against ground stations, rain gauges and satellite: models systematically **under-predict extreme rainfall**, produce divergent cyclone tracks, degrade at mesoscale, and **reanalysis-based benchmarks overstate forecast skill relative to real observations** [E]. **[I] Any Nepali team deploying a global AI weather model operationally without local observational validation is building on a benchmark illusion.**

**Gap:** no peer-reviewed cloudburst or flash-flood nowcasting study specific to Nepal was found. Nearby work is Indian-Himalaya cloudburst ANNs and Bangladeshi hilly flash-flood ML, none transferred to Nepal [E — absence].

## 5.2 Landslide

**Susceptibility.** Nepal-specific ML work is now reasonably common. Gurung et al. (2026, *J. Mountain Science*) compared RF and XGBoost on 439 mapped landslides in the Bhotekoshi watershed; RF marginally better, but the finding that matters is that **susceptibility-only mapping underestimated exposed population by more than 20× — from 360 to 7,743 once runout was modelled** [E]. **[I] Susceptibility maps without runout are a systematic underestimate of who is at risk, and Nepal's planning currently uses susceptibility maps.**

Bhattarai & Bhandary (2025, *Applied Sciences*) built an RF ensemble for co-seismic susceptibility from the 2015 Gorkha earthquake: 87.9% accuracy, AUC 0.94, and showed that **adding post-seismic rainfall substantially improves prediction** [E] — evidence that single-hazard models miss compound triggering. Muñoz-Torrero Manchado et al. (2022, *CATENA*) found prior deforestation raises landslide probability ~16% in Far-Western Nepal [E].

**Nowcasting.** NASA's LHASA v2 (Stanley et al. 2021, *Frontiers in Earth Science*) replaced v1's simple threshold with XGBoost over IMERG rainfall, SMAP soil moisture/snow, slope, fault distance and lithology; true-positive rate rose from 38% to 98% on training inventories and 45% to 93% on held-out validation — **but multitemporal-catalogue performance, the most realistic test, is only 17–34% TPR, and there is no independent Nepal/HKH validation** [E]. IMERG's underestimation of intense mesoscale convection is flagged by the authors as a key limitation [E].

**Detection.** Landslide4Sense (Ghorbanzadeh et al. 2022) is the standard benchmark: 3,799 Sentinel-2 + ALOS PALSAR DEM/slope patches from Iburi, Kodagu, Taiwan **and Gorkha, Nepal** — including a Rasuwa District study area with GPS-surveyed and VHR-interpreted labels; ResU-Net performed best among 11 architectures [E]. **[I] This is directly reusable Nepali training data and is probably the most under-exploited existing asset for a Nepali team.**

**Slow-moving deformation.** InSAR + ML detection of slope deformation is active (Faster R-CNN and U-Net over deformation-rate maps, 2025) but **no Nepal-specific InSAR slow-landslide study was found** [E — absence], despite Nepal's road corridors being an obvious target.

## 5.3 GLOF and cryosphere

Mool, Joshi & Bajracharya (2001, ICIMOD) catalogued 3,252 glaciers and 2,323 glacial lakes manually [E]. Khadka, Zhang & Thakuri (2018, *Remote Sensing*) semi-automated NDWI + Otsu mapping over Landsat 1977–2017, finding a 25% area increase and 511 new lakes since 1987, but requiring extensive manual correction [E]. Zhang et al. (2026, *J. Hydrology*) present a deep-learning framework for glacial-lake mapping across the whole HKH [E] — the automation that Khadka et al. lacked.

**The conceptual point Nepal must internalise** comes from Nurakynov et al. (2025, *Geosciences*): nearly all published "AI for GLOF" work is **monitoring** (lake-area/volume change detection, ~90% accuracy in clear conditions but 20–30% error under debris cover or shadow), **not prediction** of outburst timing or probability; genuine triggering-prediction models remain rare, hydrodynamic-simulation-dependent, and largely unvalidated against real outbursts [E].

**[I] Combined with the Thame 2024 case (a lake below the screening threshold) and the 2026 event (a source slope outside Nepal), the literature and the events agree: GLOF *prediction* is not available, GLOF *monitoring* is available but mis-scoped, and GLOF *detection at the moment of failure* is the unclaimed ground.**

## 5.4 Earthquake

**Detection and phase-picking.** EQTransformer (Mousavi et al. 2020, *Nature Communications*) — a 56-layer attention+BiLSTM multi-task network — achieves near-zero false-negative/false-positive rates on 113k test windows, detects 2.4× more events than manual catalogues in a Japan test region, and picks phases with ~0.06 s P-wave MAE. Its authors flag degraded performance at larger epicentral distances, for smaller magnitudes, and untested generalisation outside US/Japan-heavy training regions [E]. PhaseNet is the other standard baseline.

**Rapid loss.** USGS PAGER (Wald et al. 2008; Jaiswal & Wald 2010) issues fatality and economic-loss alerts within ~30 minutes of any global M>5.5 event by combining ShakeMap, population exposure and country-calibrated empirical models; its stated weaknesses are stale building inventories and ShakeMap uncertainty propagation [E].

**Prediction.** Geller et al. (1997, *Science*) — "Earthquakes Cannot Be Predicted" — remains the standard statement that no precursor method shows skill beyond chance at operationally useful lead times. **Nothing in the AI literature reviewed here claims otherwise; every result concerns post-rupture detection or post-shaking estimation** [E].

**Nepal-specific damage ML.** Göçer et al. (2025, *Buildings*) trained gradient boosting on the post-Gorkha survey (762,094 buildings, 127 variables; 27,962 RC subset), reaching 98% (3-class) and 93% (5-class) damage-grade accuracy — **but with 830 severely-damaged versus 24,530 low-damage RC buildings, an imbalance that almost certainly inflates apparent accuracy on the majority class**, and with results from a single event [E/I].

## 5.5 Computer vision for disaster response

**xBD/xView2** (Gupta et al. 2019, CVPRW): ~700,000 building annotations, 5,000+ km², 15 countries, 4-level damage ordinal scale — the reference dataset. **It does not include the 2015 Nepal earthquake**; its 19 disasters are Guatemala, four hurricanes, five fire complexes, Palu and Sunda Strait tsunamis, Mexico City, Midwest floods, three tornado events and Lower Puna. It *does* include a "monsoon" category (Jul–Sep 2017) covering the Nepal/India/Bangladesh flood region — flood imagery, not earthquake [E].

**xFBD** (2022/23) is the essential follow-up: top xView2-competition networks suffer noticeable performance drops under localised, context-independent damage judgement, because they lean on surrounding-building context rather than the building itself [E]. **[I] Models trained on xBD will likely underperform on Nepali vernacular masonry and RC construction by more than their published scores suggest.**

**Sen1Floods11** (Bonafilia et al. 2020, CVPRW): 4,831 Sentinel-1 chips, 11 flood events, 6 continents, comparing FCNs on hand labels versus Landsat/Sentinel-2-derived pseudo-labels against backscatter thresholding [E]. SAR's cloud penetration is decisive for monsoon Nepal; its layover/shadow behaviour in mountains is the countervailing limitation (§9).

**Geospatial foundation models** are the most important recent development for a label-poor country. Prithvi (Jakubik et al. 2023; NASA–IBM), a 100M-parameter ViT pretrained on >1 TB of Harmonized Landsat-Sentinel data, fine-tunes efficiently for flood mapping, burn-scar segmentation and crop monitoring with performance holding under substantially reduced labels [E]. **A direct regional test exists**: Shukla et al. (2025, IGARSS) fine-tuned Prithvi-100M for **wildfire-scar segmentation in Himalayan terrain**, finding 2- and 3-band inputs nearly match full 6-band accuracy, while flagging complex topography and scarce local labels as persistent obstacles [E]. Also relevant: Scale-MAE (Reed et al. 2023, ICCV; 2.4–5.6% kNN gains across 8 RS datasets), Presto (Tseng et al. 2023; lightweight pixel-timeseries transformer competitive with far larger models at much lower compute — **well matched to Nepal's compute budget** [I]), Clay, and TerraMind (Jakubik et al. 2025, ICCV; 9-modality generative EO model, SOTA on PANGAEA).

**UAV.** Person/crowd detection for search-and-rescue from drones has many 2024–25 papers, but they are lab-scale; **no field-deployed, peer-reviewed South-Asian UAV disaster-response study was found** [E — absence]. **[I] This absence should temper any proposal built on drone-based victim detection.**

## 5.6 Crisis NLP

CrisisNLP (Imran, Mitra & Castillo 2016, LREC) is directly Nepal-relevant: 52 million crisis tweets across 19 events **including 4.2 million from the 2015 Nepal earthquake**, annotated for 9 UN-OCHA-aligned information types. Classical classifiers reached ≥0.80 AUC on most categories but **only 0.23–0.89 on rare classes such as "missing/trapped people"** — the highest-stakes, lowest-frequency category [E]. Crucially, **the Nepal collection is distributed as tweet IDs and is English-language**; it captures international and diaspora reaction, not Nepali-language ground-level communication [E].

CrisisMMD (Alam et al. 2018) added paired text+image labels; HumAID (Alam et al. 2021) delivered deep-learning benchmarks over ~19 events; MEDIC (Alam et al. 2021) consolidated 71,198 images across 4 tasks with EfficientNet-b1 F1 of 80.7 (disaster type), 87.7 (informativeness), 84.0 (humanitarian category), 80.4 (damage severity) — **but "other disaster" scored 27.0 F1 and annotator agreement was only 46–71%** [E]. **[I] Those agreement figures are a warning about how much of reported crisis-NLP performance is label noise.**

AIDR (QCRI) was the pioneering real-time crowd+AI triage pipeline; **it is now effectively legacy — the site shows no activity after 2013–2015** [E]. **[I] There is no maintained successor filling the real-time social-media triage role for a country like Nepal.**

Geoparsing: TAGGS (de Bruijn et al. 2017) groups tweets to improve global disaster geoparsing — a reusable method, **never tested on Nepali place names or Devanagari** [E].

LLM sitrep generation: DisasterResponseGPT (Goecks & Waytowich 2023) generates action plans "comparable to human-generated ones" but is explicitly preliminary with **no hallucination evaluation** [E].

**Absence findings [E]:** no Nepali-language crisis-NLP paper; no disaster-specific misinformation-detection paper that could be verified in this review.

## 5.7 Multimodal

CrisisMMD remains the standard text+image testbed. The consistent finding across the fusion literature (attention fusion, CrisisKAN, cross-modal evidential fusion, 2022–25) is that **fusion typically edges out the best single modality by a modest margin, and the gain shrinks further when either modality is noisy** — which is the norm in real disaster social media rather than curated benchmarks [E/I].

Remote-sensing VLMs: RemoteCLIP (Liu et al. 2023, IEEE TGRS) achieved 6.39% average zero-shot classification and 8.9–9.1% retrieval gains over CLIP by building a 12× larger RS caption corpus [E]. GeoChat (Kuckreja et al. 2024, CVPR) adds region-grounded dialogue and referring-object detection for RS imagery, and its authors explicitly note that **general-purpose VLMs fabricate information on remote-sensing scenes without domain fine-tuning** [E]. EarthGPT, SkySenseGPT and LHRS-Bot exist in the survey literature but their benchmark numbers could not be independently verified in this review and are therefore named without performance claims.

**[I] The operational implication for Nepal is sharp: do not point an off-the-shelf multimodal LLM at Nepali disaster imagery and believe the output. Measure the hallucination rate first — that measurement is itself a publishable contribution (§22).**

## 5.8 Response optimisation

Pu et al. (2025, arXiv:2505.03979) review the integration of optimisation (MIP, stochastic/robust programming, VRP/OP for SAR), ML and simulation for disaster response, and identify **no ML research on facility location and no simulation work on search-and-rescue** as gaps, plus an interpretability problem limiting operational trust [E].

Liu & Meidani (2024, arXiv:2501.09803) built GNN-SDE, estimating shortest evacuation distances under flood-degraded road networks with <2% MAPE and 10× speedup over Dijkstra on graphs up to 100,000 nodes (Manhattan, Hillsborough County) [E]. Limitations: assumes uniform flooding speed reduction, requires retraining on topology change, and **has never been tested on a mountain road network where a single landslide severs the only corridor** [E/I] — a structurally different disruption pattern from urban flooding.

## 5.9 Nepal-specific AI/DRR literature: what exists and what does not

**Exists [E]:** flood susceptibility ML for the Karnali basin (Duwal et al. 2023) and Mohana-Khutiya river (Maharjan et al. 2024); landslide susceptibility for Bhotekoshi (Gurung et al. 2026) and co-seismic (Bhattarai & Bhandary 2025); deforestation–landslide linkage (Muñoz-Torrero Manchado et al. 2022); ICIMOD/Khadka glacial-lake inventories; Landslide4Sense's Gorkha/Rasuwa imagery; post-Gorkha building-damage ML (Göçer et al. 2025); CrisisNLP's 4.2M Nepal-earthquake English tweets; a Prithvi fine-tune for Himalayan wildfire scars (Shukla et al. 2025).

**Does not exist [E — absence]:** any operationally deployed Nepal flood-forecasting LSTM; any Nepali-language crisis-NLP model or corpus; any field-validated UAV disaster-response study in Nepal; any Nepal InSAR slow-landslide study; any GLOF *prediction* model validated against a real Nepali outburst; any Nepal building-level damage-label dataset; any Nepal road-blockage dataset; any published Nepali geoparsing resource.

## 5.11 The research gaps, stated as researchable problems

1. **Ungauged-basin flood LSTM regionalised for Himalayan catchments.** Nearing et al.'s architecture has no HKH validation; retrain and benchmark it on Nepal's sub-catchments with DHM discharge plus IMERG/ERA5 forcing, against the explicit limitation Nevo et al. state.
2. **Sub-daily validation of satellite rainfall against Nepal's gauge network.** LHASA v2 and the MAUSAM monsoon evaluation both name satellite underestimation of intense convection as a root-cause limitation for flood *and* landslide EWS in Nepal. Nobody has quantified it at sub-daily resolution for Nepal.
3. **A Himalayan cloudburst/flash-flood nowcasting benchmark.** No dataset comparable to Sen1Floods11 or Landslide4Sense exists for mountain-front convective extremes.
4. **Compound rainfall-after-earthquake landslide triggering as a general model**, extending Bhattarai & Bhandary's single-event finding.
5. **A national InSAR slow-moving landslide inventory for Nepal's road corridors** — the method exists (China/Europe), the application does not.
6. **ML-based GLOF hazard-probability estimation validated against real Nepali outbursts**, addressing Nurakynov et al.'s monitoring/prediction gap.
7. **A Nepali-language and code-mixed crisis-NLP dataset and model.** Nothing exists. This is the largest single vacuum in the review.
8. **Quantify how many local labels a geospatial foundation model needs** to match from-scratch CNNs on Nepal flood and landslide segmentation — extending Shukla et al.'s Himalayan wildfire proof to the hazards that kill people.
9. **Class-imbalance-corrected building damage models** for the rare "severe" class that actually drives triage.
10. **A hallucination audit of general-purpose VLMs on Nepali disaster imagery**, following GeoChat's own warning.
11. **Mountain single-corridor evacuation and logistics routing**, adapting GNN routing to a topology where one landslide severs the only road.
12. **Does multimodal fusion actually beat the best single modality on real, noisy Nepali disaster content?** The benchmark literature says gains are marginal on clean data; nobody has tested the noisy, low-resolution, code-mixed case.
13. **Seismic detection of mass-movement flood precursors in the Himalaya.** The 26 August 2026 event produced a landslide-generated seismic signal that USGS identified [E]. Nobody has built a real-time classifier for this class of signal on the Nepali network. **This is the gap with the clearest line to lives saved.** [I]

## 6.2 What Nepal already gets for free — and is not using

**[I] The pattern is unambiguous: Nepal is well served by reactive, satellite-driven, internationally-triggered tooling and poorly served by locally-owned, anticipatory, last-mile tooling.**

Within ~24 hours of 26 August 2026, without any Nepali institution building anything: GDACS auto-scored Red; the International Charter delivered 18 imagery products; Copernicus EMS activated; Sentinel Asia tasked; Vantor and Planet released free VHR; UNOSAT ran Sentinel-1 flood dashboards; HOT mapped ~3,000 km². Continuously and unprompted, FIRMS, LHASA v2 and GLOFAS run over Nepal at zero cost.

**The under-used asset is the open API layer.** Google's flood-forecasting API, GDACS's open feed, FIRMS alert subscriptions, LHASA v2's open nowcast and HDX's datasets are all free and machine-readable, and none of them appears to be piped into a Nepali government dashboard. Each activation still runs through ad hoc international request channels. **[I] Wiring these into BIPAD is a weeks-scale engineering task with national-scale value, and it is the correct first deliverable of any Nepal disaster-AI programme — it buys institutional credibility before any model is trained.**

## 6.3 The genuine white space

1. **No cross-border hydro-meteorological or cryospheric data-sharing with China/Tibet.** Hazards originating upstream are undetectable until they cross into Nepal, at which point warning collapses to minutes. [E]
2. **DHM's gauges measure stage, not rate-of-rise.** A cheap algorithmic fix that no off-the-shelf product supplies for Nepal's specific network. [E/I]
3. **No operational EEW**, and near-source geometry limits what one could deliver. [E]
4. **No AI-driven humanitarian logistics or routing in use in Nepal** — the Logistics Cluster remains dashboard-based. [E]
5. **510/IFRC's National Risk Watch has not been extended to Nepal**, despite Nepal running one of the region's most mature anticipatory-action programmes. An integration opportunity, not a technology gap. [E/I]
6. **No maintained successor to AIDR** for real-time citizen-report triage. [E]
7. **No standing pipeline converting Charter/Maxar imagery into building-level damage grades for Nepal.** [E]
8. **No seismic-network-based mass-movement detection anywhere in the Himalaya.** [E — absence] **This is the highest-value white space and nobody is in it.** [I]

## 8.2 The verdict

**Nepali NLP is not ready for operational disaster information extraction, and the failure is structural rather than a matter of more fine-tuning.** [I] The building blocks exist — a ~1 GB-scale pretraining corpus, several encoders, a 15,798-sentence NER set, a national ASR corpus, MT via FLORES/NLLB — but four specific breakages block deployment:

1. **The entity type that matters most is the weakest.** EverestNER's Event F1 (0.44) is roughly half its Person/Date F1 (0.88–0.91) [E]. Hazard and event entities are precisely what disaster IE must extract.
2. **The channels disaster reports actually arrive in are the ones handled worst.** Romanised Nepali (the dominant script for everyday texting) shows the largest native→romanised LLM accuracy drop of any language yet measured; Nepali ASR stays above 20–40% WER even fine-tuned, worse out-of-domain [E].
3. **There is no schema.** No Nepali dataset encodes casualty counts, damage severity, needs, hazard type, or affected population. Everything is generic PER/ORG/LOC/DATE [E].
4. **Geolocation is unsolved.** No Nepali geoparsing or gazetteer-resolution resource exists, against a genuinely unstable administrative naming system [E].

**What *is* viable today [I]:** triage-level, human-in-the-loop use — a few-shot-prompted LLM (per the RANLP 2025 finding that ~10 in-language exemplars help substantially) flagging candidate disaster-relevant Devanagari text for human review, in an AIDR-style workflow. Fully automated, romanised-inclusive, voice-inclusive, geocoded extraction is not credible with today's public resources.

## 8.3 The concrete target system

**Input:** `चितवनमा पहिरोले बाटो बन्द भएको छ, दुई परिवार फसेका छन्`

**Required output:**
```json
{
  "hazard_type": "landslide",
  "location": {"surface": "चितवन", "resolved": "Chitwan District",
                "admin_level": "district", "gazetteer_id": "NPL-BAG-CHI",
                "confidence": 0.91, "granularity_warning": "district-level only; no municipality/ward given"},
  "infrastructure": [{"type": "road", "status": "blocked", "confidence": 0.95}],
  "people_affected": {"unit": "families", "count": 2, "status": "trapped", "confidence": 0.88},
  "casualties": {"dead": null, "injured": null, "missing": null},
  "severity": "high",
  "urgency": "immediate",
  "source_reliability": "unverified_single_report",
  "language": "ne-Deva",
  "script": "devanagari"
}
```

**[I] The three hard parts are not the hazard label.** They are (i) resolving `चितवन` to an actionable administrative unit — district granularity is nearly useless for dispatch, and the message gives nothing finer; (ii) representing `दुई परिवार` (two *families*, not two people) without silently converting it to a person count; and (iii) refusing to emit a casualty number when none was stated, rather than hallucinating zero. Any evaluation that does not test these three behaviours is not measuring the thing that matters.

## 8.4 Proposed evaluation protocol

**Schema.** Extend EverestNER's five types with: HAZARD_TYPE, LOCATION (with gazetteer ID, admin level, and an explicit granularity flag), CASUALTY_COUNT (dead / injured / missing, each with numeric value and unit), INFRASTRUCTURE_DAMAGE (type, status, severity), NEED (shelter / water / medical / food / rescue), ORG (responder), DATE/TIME, SOURCE_RELIABILITY (first-hand / second-hand / rumour). Document-level frame: `{hazard_type, locations, time, casualties, needs, infrastructure, responding_orgs, source_type}`.

**Metrics.**
- Entity-level micro- **and** macro-F1, **reported per type** so that weak classes such as HAZARD_TYPE are not hidden by strong ones such as DATE. Exact-match and partial-overlap variants both, because Nepali segmentation is inconsistent.
- Slot-filling: exact match for categorical slots; **normalised numeric accuracy with tolerance bands** for counts; **geocoding accuracy at the local-unit (palika) level, not district**.
- A **null-precision** metric: the rate at which the model correctly emits `null` for slots not stated in the text. **[I] This is the single most safety-critical metric and it is absent from every crisis-NLP benchmark reviewed.**
- End-to-end "actionable report" score combining slot completeness and correctness.
- **Stratified reporting by channel and script:** (a) Devanagari native, (b) romanised, (c) code-mixed, (d) ASR-transcribed voice. The Script Gap evidence makes it indefensible to report a single pooled number.
- Robustness under spelling variation, dialect and social-media noise — every existing Nepali corpus is news-domain and will overstate real-world performance.

**Baselines.** (a) Rule-based gazetteer lookup; (b) fine-tuned NepBERTa and XLM-R sequence taggers; (c) few-shot-prompted frontier LLM under the RANLP-2025 Least-to-Most protocol, in both scripts; (d) a cascade (romanised → transliterated to Devanagari → native-script IE).

**Annotation quality.** Double annotation with adjudication; report token-level Cohen's κ for spans and Krippendorff's α for structured slots; require κ ≥ 0.7 before trusting the gold set; and **measure agreement separately on romanised items**, since the Script Gap findings suggest human and model judgements both diverge more there.

## 9.2 What will not work in Nepal, with evidence

**SAR geometric distortion disables a large share of Nepal's terrain.** Side-looking C-band SAR over steep slopes suffers layover, foreshortening and radar shadow — a documented failure mode requiring dedicated layover-detection algorithms in mountainous settings [E — Zhao et al. 2021, *Remote Sensing*]. Because Nepal is predominantly hill and mountain terrain, **single-geometry Sentinel-1 flood and landslide products are structurally biased toward valley bottoms and the Terai** [I]. Combining ascending and descending passes fills some shadow and layover gaps but **cannot recover foreshortened or fully layover-affected pixels**, and doubles acquisition cost and latency [E/I]. UN-SPIDER's recommended practice for Sentinel-1/2 flood mapping is written around lowland floodplain geometry, not Himalayan valleys [E].

**Optical imagery is unusable over much of Nepal in exactly the months disasters occur.** Analysis of the 2015 Gorkha response found that mountainous parts of the affected region waited an average of **~16.8 days for a cloud-free image** versus ~1–2 days in Kathmandu Valley — and that **much of the mountainous north is expected to have no cloud-free days at all between June and September** [E]. **[I] Any Nepal EO plan whose critical path runs through Sentinel-2 during the monsoon is invalid on its face.**

**10 m pixels miss the landslides that kill people.** The fatal, road-severing failures in Nepal's Middle Hills are frequently narrow, shallow, translational slides tens of metres wide — at or below Sentinel-2's detection floor. The Sentinel-2 landslide-detection literature is validated mainly on larger contiguous slides in Taiwan/China/Japan-type terrain, and transferability studies report generalisation loss on smaller or differently-shaped slides [E]. Nepal-specific satellite-derived inventories rely on higher-resolution imagery precisely for this reason [E].

**InSAR degrades badly in vegetated, steep, monsoon-affected terrain.** Coherence loss from dense vegetation, snow and heavy rainfall breaks the interferometric time series; PS-InSAR applied to Himalayan slopes requires careful point-density and atmospheric correction and is treated in the literature as a bespoke, non-default use case [E]. **[I] It works for slow creep on exposed rock and debris; it does not reliably catch sudden rainfall-triggered failures on forested hillsides — the majority of Nepal's fatal landslide type.**

**Terai flood algorithms do not transfer to mountain valleys, and vice versa.** Terai flooding presents broad, low-gradient inundation with strong open-water backscatter contrast [E]; mountain flash floods and debris-laden surges present mixed-pixel, high-turbulence surfaces that defeat the smooth low-backscatter signature SAR flood algorithms are tuned for, in valleys often near or below sensor resolution. **[I] These are two different remote-sensing problems and must not be addressed by "one Nepal flood model."**

**Snow, ice and debris cover defeat naive water indices.** Lake ice, debris cover and turbid meltwater all break NDWI-style thresholds; small newly forming lakes are routinely missed by inventories imposing minimum-size thresholds — **exactly the failure that left the 2024 Thame lake off every hazard list** [E].

**Ground truth is chronically sparse**, which is the core motivation for foundation-model transfer (§9.4) [E/I].

## 9.3 DEMs for Nepal

SRTM 30 m, ASTER GDEM, **Copernicus GLO-30**, ALOS AW3D30, **FABDEM** (canopy- and building-height-corrected), TanDEM-X, and UAV photogrammetry. Recent work shows **DEM choice materially changes slope-unit delineation in landslide susceptibility** [E — NHESS 2025]. **[I] Recommendation: FABDEM or Copernicus GLO-30 as the national default for hydrological and landslide modelling, SRTM only for backward compatibility, and UAV photogrammetry for specific at-risk moraine dams and settlement sites.** A national high-resolution DEM from the Survey Department could not be confirmed as publicly downloadable, which is why practically all published Nepal EO work uses global DEMs [E].

## 9.4 The methods that cope with Nepal's constraints

1. **SAR change detection and coherence loss** — the only all-weather signal, and the only route to damage/flood information during the monsoon.
2. **Ascending + descending pass fusion**, accepting the residual layover loss and *reporting the unobservable fraction* rather than hiding it. **[I] A "terrain observability mask" published alongside any Nepal SAR flood product is a small, genuinely useful research contribution nobody has made.**
3. **Optical + SAR fusion** on the rare cloud-free windows.
4. **Geospatial foundation models to reduce label demand** — Prithvi, Presto, Clay, TerraMind, Scale-MAE, DOFA. The Himalayan wildfire-scar Prithvi fine-tune is a direct regional precedent [E].
5. **Domain adaptation and weak/noisy-label learning** for out-of-region generalisation, given xFBD's demonstrated OOD collapse [E].
6. **Explicit avoidance of super-resolution as a substitute for resolution.** [I] Upsampling a 10 m Sentinel-2 scene does not create the information needed to detect a 30 m landslide; treating it as if it does is a recurring failure in low-resource EO work.

## 9.6 UAV in Nepal: the regulatory and practical reality

CAAN requires registration for drones over 250 g and a Remote Pilot Certificate for commercial operation (18+, written exam, 10+ supervised flight hours, medical certificate, 2-year renewal); flight within 5 km of Tribhuvan International Airport is banned; national parks and wildlife reserves require special permits; and mandatory Remote ID is being introduced with enforcement infrastructure still developing [E]. Post-2015, small fixed-wing UAVs were used for structural damage mapping and heritage documentation with clear practical value — **but this required foreign operator involvement, equipment import/customs friction, and terrain-constrained launch and recovery** [E].

**[I] The realistic 2026 model is a trained in-country pilot corps holding pre-approved CAAN disaster-response clearance, not ad hoc foreign deployment. Any project whose critical path assumes drones will fly within 24 hours of a disaster should treat that clearance as a milestone to be secured in advance, not an operational detail.**

# 10. Multimodal AI Opportunities

## 10.1 The honest prior

The fusion literature's consistent finding is that **text+image fusion typically beats the best single modality by a modest margin, and the gain shrinks when either modality is noisy** [E/I]. Real Nepali disaster content is *maximally* noisy: blurry phone photos, terse code-mixed captions, romanised script, duplicated forwards. **[I] Therefore every multimodal system proposed below must be evaluated against strong single-modality baselines, and a result showing that fusion does not help in the Nepali case is a publishable and operationally valuable finding, not a failure.** This is research gap #12 in §5.11.

## 10.2 Five concrete system concepts

### Concept A — SURGE-WATCH: seismo-hydrological cascade detection (the flagship)
**Modalities:** continuous seismic waveforms (NK network via FDSN/IRIS + regional stations) + river-gauge time series at native sampling + multi-temporal satellite lake/slope monitoring + a static exposure layer.
**Fusion:** late fusion of three independent detectors into a Bayesian confidence score, with an explicit corroboration requirement (a seismic detection alone raises a watch; a seismic detection *plus* a downstream rate-of-rise anomaly raises an alert).
**Output:** a CAP-XML alert naming affected river reach, estimated arrival times at named settlements (from a pre-computed travel-time table), exposed population from WorldPop/census, and a confidence level.
**Why multimodal is essential [I]:** the seismic channel is the only one that sees a *dry-day, cross-border* initiation; the gauge channel is the only one that confirms water is actually moving; the satellite channel is the only one that provides pre-event context. No single modality suffices, and the corroboration requirement is what controls false alarms.
**Precedent:** none in the Himalaya. This is genuinely new work. [E — absence]

### Concept B — TRIAGE: citizen-report to rescue-priority map
**Modalities:** Nepali/romanised/code-mixed text (social media, SMS, hotline transcripts) + user-submitted images + Sentinel-1 flood extent + OSM road graph + WorldPop.
**Pipeline:** NLP extracts hazard type, location surface form, people affected, needs, urgency (§8.3 schema) → geoparser resolves to palika/ward → CV classifies attached images for damage severity and hazard confirmation → cross-check against the SAR-derived flood/landslide extent → deduplicate reports by (space, time, content) similarity → rank by exposed population × severity × corroboration.
**Output:** "High-priority zone: Barhabise municipality ward 4 — 3 roads blocked (2 corroborated by imagery) — ~1,200 people exposed — nearest accessible shelter: Sukute, reachable via the north bank."
**Key design constraint [I]:** *corroboration is the product.* A report ranked highly because three independent channels agree is actionable; a report ranked highly because one viral tweet was retweeted 400 times is dangerous. Deduplication and source-independence weighting are the core engineering, not the classifier.

### Concept C — SADAK-GRAPH: road accessibility under cascading blockage
**Modalities:** VHR/UAV imagery (blockage detection) + Sentinel-1 flood/landslide extent + OSM/HOT road graph + NLP over NDRRMA/police situation reports and news + DEM.
**Method:** CV and NLP each produce candidate blockage events with confidence; these are fused as edge-removal probabilities on the road graph; a GNN or probabilistic shortest-path model then estimates travel time and reachability from each district headquarters and hospital to each ward, producing an "isolation index."
**Why this matters for Nepal specifically [I]:** the published GNN routing work assumes uniform flood-driven speed reduction on dense urban grids. Nepal's failure mode is a **single landslide severing the only corridor** — a discrete, high-impact, topologically different event. Modelling that is a genuine methodological contribution, not an application of an existing method.

### Concept D — DAMAGE-GRADE: post-earthquake structural triage for Nepali construction
**Modalities:** pre/post VHR optical + Sentinel-1 coherence loss (all-weather backstop) + UAV oblique imagery + building footprints (VIDA) + NSET-derived typology priors + NLP over field damage-assessment reports.
**Method:** an xBD-pretrained backbone fine-tuned on Nepal-specific labels created from the 2015 and 2023 events, with **cost-sensitive learning or targeted oversampling for the rare severe-damage class** (correcting the 830-versus-24,530 imbalance in the existing Nepali work), and explicit OOD evaluation following xFBD's protocol.
**Honest caveat [I]:** this requires creating Nepal building-damage labels that do not currently exist. It is a data project wearing a model project's clothes, and should be budgeted as such.

### Concept E — MISSING-LINK: multilingual missing-person and casualty reconciliation
**Modalities:** Nepali/romanised/English text from police lists, hospital admissions, holding-centre registers, family social-media posts, embassy enquiries + optionally face images where families consent + geographic priors.
**Method:** multilingual name normalisation (Devanagari ↔ romanised ↔ English transliteration variants, which for Nepali names is a genuinely hard many-to-many problem), fuzzy record linkage with blocking on district and approximate age, human-in-the-loop adjudication, and an auditable provenance chain for every merge.
**Why now [I]:** the August 2026 event created a register of **4,606 unaccounted people including 583 foreign nationals from 38 countries**, distributed across holding centres in four districts, with figures unreconciled for weeks. This is the most immediately useful NLP application in Nepal today and nobody has built it.
**Severe ethical constraint:** see §19. Face matching on disaster victims is a category of system that can cause real harm; the default design should be text-only, opt-in, government-hosted, and non-public.

## 10.3 Multimodal design rules for Nepal

| Rule | Rationale |
|---|---|
| Always report single-modality baselines alongside the fused model | Fusion gains are modest and shrink under noise [E] |
| Require **source independence** for corroboration, not just agreement | Two retweets of the same claim are one piece of evidence |
| Never let a generative model invent a field value | GeoChat's authors report general VLMs fabricate on RS scenes [E] |
| Degrade gracefully to the surviving modality | Four of five gauges were destroyed on 26 Aug 2026 [E] |
| Stratify all metrics by script and channel | Script Gap evidence [E] |
| Publish the observability mask | Terrain and cloud make part of Nepal unobservable; hiding that is misleading [I] |

# 11. Geospatial AI Opportunities

## 11.1 Where GIS + ML genuinely helps

1. **Slope-unit susceptibility with runout**, not pixel-wise susceptibility alone — the Bhotekoshi study's 360 → 7,743 exposure revision is the strongest single argument in the Nepali literature for changing current practice [E].
2. **Population and building exposure at ward level**, using WorldPop, VIDA buildings and 2021 census ward aggregates — all publicly available today [E].
3. **Road-network graph models** for accessibility, isolation and rescue-route planning (§10.2 Concept C).
4. **Hydrological model + ML hybrids** (HEC-HMS / LISFLOOD-FP coupled with learned residual correction) for catchments where a physical model exists but is poorly calibrated.
5. **Terrain-aware learning** — feeding slope, aspect, curvature, and an explicit SAR-observability mask as model inputs rather than treating imagery as if terrain were flat.

## 11.2 The Nepal Disaster Knowledge Graph: a qualified yes

**Precedents [E]:** earthquake event knowledge graphs with entity-relation extraction for post-earthquake rescue coordination; KG + case-based reasoning for hydraulic-engineering emergencies; LLM-assisted KG construction for typhoon power-grid emergencies; KG+LLM emergency decision frameworks; a many-objective emergency decision-making KG. **These are almost entirely Chinese-institution research prototypes, single-hazard, built on structured emergency-plan text plus sensor feeds.** No EventKG-style, continuously updated, open disaster knowledge graph is in production anywhere, and none targets the Himalaya [E].

**Verdict [I]:** a knowledge graph is a defensible **integration and explanation layer**, not a sensing solution. It adds nothing to the physics problems of §9.2. It is worth building only as the layer that fuses EO-derived hazard products, infrastructure graphs, exposure layers and administrative metadata into queryable, *explainable* relationships — the kind that answers "which health posts become unreachable if this road segment is blocked, and how many people does that isolate?"

**Proposed entity and relation schema:**

```
ENTITIES
  Hazard(id, type, onset_time, confidence, source_modality)
  SatelliteObservation(id, sensor, acquisition_time, footprint, observability_mask)
  SeismicEvent(id, origin_time, station_set, classification, non_tectonic_score)
  GaugeReading(id, station, time, stage, rate_of_rise, anomaly_score)
  WeatherEvent(id, type, time_window, intensity, source)
  GlacialLake(id, geometry, area_series, moraine_type, hazard_rank)
  River(id, reach_geometry, basin, travel_time_table)
  Location(id, admin_level, gazetteer_ids[], name_variants[])   # devanagari, romanised, english, historical VDC
  Municipality(id, province, district, ward_count)
  Village / Settlement(id, geometry, population, building_count)
  Building(id, footprint, typology, occupancy, damage_grade)
  Road(id, geometry, class, passability_state, last_verified)
  Bridge(id, geometry, type, status)
  Hospital / School / Shelter(id, geometry, capacity, access_state)
  Population(admin_unit, count, age_sex_structure, vulnerability_index)
  EmergencyReport(id, text, language, script, source_type, reliability, extracted_frame)
  Person(id, status, reported_by[], match_confidence)          # access-controlled, see §19

KEY RELATIONS
  Hazard -[AFFECTS]-> Location | Road | Building | Population
  Hazard -[DETECTED_BY]-> SeismicEvent | GaugeReading | SatelliteObservation | EmergencyReport
  Hazard -[TRIGGERED_BY]-> WeatherEvent | Hazard      # cascade edges: the important ones
  Road -[BLOCKED_BY]-> Hazard    (probability, evidence_sources[], verified_by)
  Settlement -[REACHABLE_FROM]-> Hospital  (travel_time, confidence, valid_at)
  GlacialLake -[DRAINS_INTO]-> River -[FLOWS_THROUGH]-> Settlement (travel_time)
  EmergencyReport -[CORROBORATES | CONTRADICTS]-> EmergencyReport
  Location -[ALIAS_OF]-> Location                     # the 3,900 VDC → 753 palika problem
```

**[I] Two design points make this Nepal-specific rather than generic.** First, `Location.name_variants` and `ALIAS_OF` edges are not decoration — they are the mechanism that makes Nepali geoparsing tractable across Devanagari, romanised, English and pre-2015 VDC names. Second, `TRIGGERED_BY` edges between Hazards make cascade reasoning first-class, which is precisely what the 2014, 2021, 2025 and 2026 events all required and no Nepali system currently supports.

**What it enables:** explainable decision support ("this alert fired because a non-tectonic seismic event was classified upstream at 08:39 and the Timure gauge showed a 2.1 m rise in 4 minutes at 08:47"), automatic exposure computation, isolation indices, and an audit trail — which §17 and §19 argue is a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have.

**What it does not do:** detect anything. Build it second, on top of detectors that work.

## 12.1 Project GHADI — Sub-Hourly Detection of Cascading Flood Surges in Transboundary Himalayan Rivers

*(घडी — "clock/watch"; the point of the system is time.)*

**Research problem.** Nepal's deadliest 2026 disaster had no meteorological precursor, originated outside Nepali territory, and destroyed four of five downstream gauges before their stage thresholds triggered. Every operational warning system covering Nepal — GLOFAS, Google Flood Hub, LHASA v2, DHM's own bulletins — is rainfall- or stage-threshold-driven and was therefore structurally blind [E]. Yet the initiating mass movement **produced a seismic signal strong enough that USGS initially catalogued it as an M4.4 earthquake before identifying it as landslide-generated** [E]. That signal existed, in public, on open FDSN data, minutes before the water arrived — and nobody was listening for it.

**Hypothesis [H].** A classifier operating on continuous waveform data from Nepal's NK network and neighbouring regional stations can detect large mass-movement events (ice-rock avalanche, rock slide, debris flow, landslide-dam failure) in Himalayan headwaters within **60–180 seconds** of initiation, at a false-alarm rate low enough for operational use when required to corroborate with an independent downstream rate-of-rise anomaly; and the resulting alert, emitted as CAP and fired through Nepal's existing SMS/siren infrastructure, would deliver **10–35 minutes of actionable warning** to settlements in the upper Bhote Koshi–Trishuli, Sun Koshi, Arun, Tamakoshi and Karnali corridors.

**Research questions.**
- **RQ1.** Can non-tectonic mass-movement seismic signals in the Nepal Himalaya be reliably discriminated from earthquakes, quarry blasts, and cultural noise on a sparse network? What detection latency and what false-alarm rate per station-month are achievable?
- **RQ2.** How much does the *location and depth* of a detection need to be constrained for a warning to be actionable? Is "an anomalous mass movement occurred somewhere in this 30 km river reach" sufficient, given that the alert can be scoped to a river corridor rather than a point?
- **RQ3.** What is the marginal detection value of adding gauge rate-of-rise anomaly detection, and what is the optimal corroboration rule under an explicit cost-loss framework (a missed event costs lives; a false alarm costs trust)?
- **RQ4.** What lead time is actually delivered, end to end, from initiation to a mobile phone ringing — and where does the latency budget go?
- **RQ5.** Does adding continuous satellite monitoring of source-zone slopes and lakes (including inside Tibet, from open imagery) measurably raise prior probability in a way that improves the detector's operating point?

**Target disasters.** GLOF and cryospheric cascade; landslide-dam outburst floods; large rock/ice avalanches; debris flows. Secondarily, rapid post-earthquake landslide detection.

**AI methods.** A 1D CNN / transformer event classifier over three-component waveforms (EQTransformer- and PhaseNet-class architectures retrained for the mass-movement-versus-earthquake discrimination task, exploiting the known signal differences: emergent onset, long duration, low-frequency-dominated, no clear P/S separation); sequence anomaly detection (changepoint + LSTM/temporal-convolution residual models) on gauge series; a Bayesian late-fusion layer producing a calibrated alert probability; and a rules layer emitting CAP-XML. Optional: a lightweight time-series foundation model for the gauge channel.

**NLP component.** Secondary but real: (i) parse DHM bulletins, NDRRMA situation reports, ICIMOD advisories and news for corroboration and for post-event ground truth; (ii) automatically generate the human-readable CAP `<description>` and `<instruction>` fields in Nepali and English from the structured detection, with a strict template and no free generation of numbers.

**CV component.** Multi-temporal satellite monitoring of source-zone glacial lakes and unstable slopes (Sentinel-1/2, Landsat archive, NISAR when coherent), producing a slowly-varying prior; plus post-event surge-extent mapping from Sentinel-1 for validation and for the event catalogue.

**Datasets required.**
- *Existing:* NK network continuous waveforms via FDSN/IRIS (open, back to 1978 for some stations) [E]; regional Indian and Chinese stations available through FDSN where shared; DHM River Watch series (access to be negotiated); USGS event catalogue including the misclassified 26 Aug 2026 signal; ICIMOD glacial lake inventory; Copernicus EMS EMSR927 and Planet crisis imagery for the 2026 event; Landslide4Sense for slope context.
- *New:* **A Himalayan Mass-Movement Seismic Event Catalogue** — the core new dataset. Waveform windows labelled for known events (Aug 2026 Bhote Koshi; Jul 2025 Rasuwagadhi; Aug 2024 Thame; Jun 2021 Melamchi; Aug 2014 Jure; 1985 Dig Tsho if any records survive; plus the 4,312+ Gorkha-triggered landslides, which give a large co-seismic training set with satellite-derived timing bounds), plus a large negative set of earthquakes and noise. **[I] The Gorkha landslide population is the key asset that makes this project tractable — it is the only Himalayan mass-movement event set large enough to train on, and it has published satellite-derived inventories to anchor labels.**

**Input.** Continuous three-component seismic waveforms; DHM gauge stage at native sampling; static basin travel-time tables; exposure layers.
**Output.** A calibrated detection with (time, river reach, confidence, corroborating channels) → CAP-XML alert with estimated arrival times at named settlements and exposed population.

**Evaluation metrics.** Detection: probability of detection and false-alarm rate per station-month, with a full ROC and an explicit operating point chosen under a stated cost-loss ratio; **detection latency distribution (the headline metric — median and 95th percentile seconds from initiation)**; localisation error along the river reach. End-to-end: **simulated lead time at each settlement** against reconstructed surge travel times for the 2026 and 2025 events. Reliability: calibration curves; graceful-degradation performance with N stations removed (mandatory, given four gauges were destroyed).

**Baselines.** (i) Current practice — stage-threshold alerting, i.e. zero warning for the 2026 event; (ii) simple STA/LTA seismic detection; (iii) rate-of-rise threshold on gauges alone; (iv) an off-the-shelf EQTransformer without mass-movement retraining.

**Novelty.** No real-time seismic mass-movement detection system exists anywhere in the Himalaya [E — absence]. The literature on seismic detection of landslides and debris flows exists in the Alps, Taiwan and the US, but nobody has built a Nepal-network classifier, nobody has fused it with hydrological rate-of-rise, and nobody has closed the loop to CAP dissemination. The **corroboration-under-cost-loss framing** and the **graceful degradation requirement** are both novel operational contributions.

**Expected contribution.** (i) The first Himalayan mass-movement seismic event catalogue; (ii) a validated detector with published latency and false-alarm characteristics; (iii) an end-to-end lead-time analysis of the August 2026 event answering the question "how many minutes could have been bought?"; (iv) an open CAP-emitting reference implementation.

**Deployment scenario.** A service running on modest hardware (or a small cloud instance with an on-prem fallback), operated by DHM with NDRRMA as the alerting authority, emitting CAP into BIPAD and into the mass-SMS pathway that already demonstrably works (679,295 messages in one minute). Human-in-the-loop gate on the first deployment tier; automatic firing only for the highest-confidence corroborated tier, and only after a validated shadow period.

**Difficulty 8. Cost:** USD 45–80 k over 18 months (2 researchers + 1 engineer part-time, modest compute, seismology travel, no new hardware required in phase 1; optional gauge-upgrade pilot adds USD 20–40 k). **Timeline:** 18–24 months to field validation; 12 months to a publishable retrospective result. **Publication potential: very high** — *Nature Communications* / *NHESS* / *Seismological Research Letters* / *Geophysical Research Letters* class for the detection science; a separate systems paper for the CAP pipeline.

**Risks and honest weaknesses [I].** (i) The number of large, well-documented Himalayan mass-movement events with good seismic records is small; the Gorkha co-seismic landslide population partly compensates but those signals are buried in the mainshock coda, which is itself a hard research problem. (ii) False alarms in a country with low institutional trust are expensive; the corroboration requirement mitigates but does not eliminate this. (iii) The project depends on DHM cooperation for gauge data at native sampling. (iv) Automated alerting sits in tension with the DRRM Regulations' prohibition on transmitting information that "may create unnecessary panic or fear" [E] — a legal question to be resolved with NDRRMA early, not late.

## 12.2 Project NEPAL-CRISIS-IE — A Nepali, Romanised and Code-Mixed Disaster Information Extraction Corpus, Model and Missing-Person Linkage System

**Research problem.** There is **no Nepali-language disaster text corpus in existence** [E]. The only Nepal crisis-NLP resource (CrisisNLP 2015) is 4.2 million *English* tweet IDs capturing diaspora reaction. The one Nepali NER dataset with an Event class scores F1 0.44 on it. Romanised Nepali — the script most Nepalis actually text in — shows the largest native→romanised LLM accuracy drop of any language yet measured (>15, up to 24 points), with 65.6% of romanised errors being outright comprehension failures. Nepali ASR remains at ~24% WER even fine-tuned. And no Nepali geoparsing resource exists at all, against an administrative naming system in which ~3,900 pre-2015 VDCs were collapsed into 753 local units whose romanisation is not even internally consistent in official documents. [E]

Meanwhile, the operational cost of this vacuum is currently visible: after the August 2026 flood, **4,606 people were unaccounted for, including 583 foreign nationals from 38 countries, across holding centres in four districts**, and casualty figures were revised daily for a week across mutually inconsistent police, army and media sources [E].

**Hypothesis [H].** (H1) A 25,000–40,000-message Nepali disaster corpus, annotated with a disaster-specific slot schema and stratified across Devanagari / romanised / code-mixed / ASR-transcribed channels, is sufficient to lift hazard-and-event extraction F1 from the ~0.44 baseline to >0.75, and to make palika-level geoparsing viable. (H2) A gazetteer-grounded, alias-aware geoparser will outperform a frontier LLM's zero-shot geolocation on Nepali place names by a wide margin, because the failure is a knowledge failure, not a reasoning failure. (H3) Multilingual name normalisation plus blocked fuzzy record linkage can reduce duplicate and unmatched entries in a multi-source missing-person register by a measurable and operationally meaningful margin against a human-annotated gold standard.

**Research questions.**
- **RQ1.** What annotation schema captures the fields Nepali responders actually act on, and can it be annotated at κ ≥ 0.7?
- **RQ2.** How much does performance degrade from Devanagari to romanised to code-mixed to ASR-transcribed input, on the *same* underlying content? (No study has measured this for a disaster domain in any language.)
- **RQ3.** Does transliterating romanised input to Devanagari before extraction beat direct romanised extraction, and does MuRIL's transliterated pretraining give it an advantage over NepBERTa here?
- **RQ4.** Can a model be taught to reliably emit `null` rather than hallucinate casualty counts? What is the null-precision of frontier LLMs on this task today?
- **RQ5.** In record linkage, what is the accuracy/recall trade-off, and where must a human adjudicator sit for the system to be safe?

**Target disasters.** All — this is horizontal infrastructure. Corpus construction anchors on the 2026 Bhote Koshi flood, the Sept 2024 floods, the 2023 Jajarkot earthquake and the 2015 Gorkha earthquake.

**AI methods.** Fine-tuned encoders (NepBERTa, MuRIL, XLM-R) for token classification and slot filling; few-shot and Least-to-Most-prompted LLMs as a strong baseline and as an annotation-assist; a transliteration model for the romanised→Devanagari cascade; retrieval-augmented generation for situation-report drafting, strictly grounded in extracted structured records; and classical blocked fuzzy matching plus a learned pairwise scorer for record linkage.

**NLP component.** The entire project.
**CV component.** Light but real: classify user-submitted images attached to reports for hazard confirmation and damage severity (MEDIC-pretrained backbone), used as a corroboration signal for text-extracted claims — and used to test the fusion question of §10.1 on genuinely noisy Nepali data.

**Datasets.**
- *Existing:* EverestNER (15,798 sentences), EBIQUITY/ILPRL NER, NepBERTa corpus, OpenSLR-54 speech, CrisisNLP Nepal English tweets (for cross-lingual comparison), MEDIC/CrisisMMD (for image pretraining), OSM Nepali name tags and the official 753-unit administrative list (for the gazetteer).
- *New (the contribution):* **NepDIS** — 25,000–40,000 annotated messages across Devanagari, romanised, code-mixed and ASR-transcribed channels, drawn from public social media, news comment streams, published situation reports, and (with authorisation) anonymised hotline transcripts; **NepGeo** — a Nepali disaster gazetteer with Devanagari, romanised, English and pre-2015 VDC alias sets for all 753 local units and major settlements; and a **held-out record-linkage evaluation set** built from published, already-public casualty and missing lists.

**Input.** Raw Nepali/romanised/code-mixed/English text, optionally with images. **Output.** The structured frame of §8.3, plus a deduplicated, linked report and person registry.

**Evaluation metrics.** As specified in §8.4 — per-type F1, slot exact-match, numeric tolerance-band accuracy, **palika-level geocoding accuracy**, **null-precision**, all **stratified by script and channel**; record linkage by pairwise precision/recall/F1 and cluster-level purity against a human gold standard; and inter-annotator κ / Krippendorff's α reported for the corpus itself.

**Baselines.** Rule-based gazetteer lookup; fine-tuned NepBERTa; fine-tuned XLM-R/MuRIL; zero-shot and few-shot frontier LLM; the transliteration cascade; and — for linkage — exact string match and standard Levenshtein/Jaro-Winkler blocking.

**Novelty.** The first Nepali crisis-NLP dataset of any kind. The first stratified script/channel degradation study in a disaster domain. The first Nepali geoparsing resource. The first formalisation of **null-precision** as a crisis-NLP safety metric. **[I] Dataset papers in low-resource NLP publish reliably; this project's floor is high even if every model result is negative.**

**Expected contribution.** An openly released corpus, gazetteer, models and evaluation protocol that every subsequent Nepali disaster-NLP effort builds on — plus an operational tool NDRRMA and Nepal Police could use in the next event.

**Deployment scenario.** Two tiers. (i) A report-triage service feeding BIPAD, human-in-the-loop, modelled on AIDR's workflow but maintained. (ii) A missing-person reconciliation tool operated *by* Nepal Police / NDRRMA on government infrastructure, never public-facing, with per-merge audit logs (see §19).

**Difficulty 6. Cost:** USD 35–60 k over 12–18 months, dominated by annotation labour (budget 4–6 annotators for 4–6 months, plus adjudication) rather than compute. **Timeline:** schema + pilot annotation by month 3; corpus v1 by month 8; models and paper by month 12; linkage tool by month 15. **Publication potential: very high** — LREC-COLING, ACL/EMNLP resource tracks, *ICWSM*, and a strong candidate for a "Resource" best-paper track given the total absence of prior work.

**Risks [I].** (i) Social-media data access is now legally and technically harder than it was in 2015; the corpus must be built with a data-source strategy that does not depend on a single platform's API. (ii) Annotation quality on romanised and code-mixed text is genuinely difficult and κ may come in below target — which is itself worth reporting. (iii) The missing-person application touches highly sensitive personal data and must not proceed without an explicit government data-sharing agreement (§19).

## 12.3 Project HIMAL-SLIDE — Nepal-Calibrated Landslide Nowcasting and a Foundation-Model National Landslide Inventory

**Research problem.** Landslides are Nepal's largest natural-hazard killer in the official record (859 deaths / 6 years) and have **no early-warning system of any kind** [E]. The global state of the art, NASA LHASA v2, achieves only **17–34% true-positive rate on multitemporal catalogues** — the realistic test — has **no published Nepal validation**, runs at 1 km resolution, and depends on IMERG rainfall that its own authors flag as underestimating intense mesoscale convection [E]. Meanwhile Nepal has only **~6,000 mapped landslides at 0.05 per km², with 40% of the country mapped only once** [E], so there is barely a ground truth to validate against. And Sentinel-2's 10 m pixels systematically miss the small, shallow, translational slides that actually kill Nepalis [E].

**Hypothesis [H].** (H1) Recalibrating an LHASA-class model on Nepal-specific rainfall thresholds, Nepal-specific susceptibility (including runout), and gauge-corrected rather than raw satellite rainfall will raise multitemporal true-positive rate materially above the 17–34% global baseline at a comparable false-alarm rate. (H2) A geospatial foundation model (Prithvi / Presto / Clay / TerraMind) fine-tuned on PlanetScope and Sentinel-2 imagery will detect sub-50 m landslides at usable precision with an order of magnitude fewer local labels than a from-scratch CNN — extending the Himalayan wildfire-scar precedent to the hazards that kill people. (H3) A 6–24 hour ward-level "high landslide risk tonight" advisory is the operationally correct product for Nepal, because minute-scale warning is neither achievable nor necessary for a hazard that kills people asleep in dwellings.

**Research questions.** How much does gauge-based bias correction of IMERG/CHIRPS improve landslide nowcast skill in Nepal, quantitatively? What is the label-efficiency curve for each candidate foundation model on Nepali landslide segmentation? What is the smallest landslide reliably detectable at each of 10 m, 3 m and 0.5 m resolution in Nepali terrain, and what fraction of fatal landslides does each threshold capture? Does adding a post-seismic-legacy covariate (distance to 2015 rupture, time since) improve prediction a decade later?

**Target disaster.** Rainfall-triggered and post-seismic landslides; secondarily debris flows.

**AI methods.** XGBoost/LightGBM for the nowcast (LHASA v2 class), with quantile regression for calibrated probabilities; U-Net / ResU-Net and foundation-model fine-tunes (Prithvi, Presto, Clay, TerraMind) for detection; slope-unit delineation on FABDEM/GLO-30; physically-based runout coupling; and self-training / weak supervision to exploit unlabelled imagery.

**NLP component.** Build the national inventory partly by **extracting landslide events from Nepali-language news archives, DRR Portal/BIPAD incident records and district reports** — dates, locations, casualties — and geocoding them with the NepGeo gazetteer from Project NEPAL-CRISIS-IE. **[I] This is the practical route to a national fatal-landslide inventory: satellite mapping finds scars, but text mining finds the ones that killed people, and the intersection is what matters for validation.** This dependency is why NEPAL-CRISIS-IE should run first or in parallel.

**CV component.** The detection half of the project.

**Datasets.** *Existing:* Landslide4Sense (with its Gorkha/Rasuwa Nepal labels), Roback et al. Gorkha inventory, NASA COOLR, Kincey et al.'s reviewed inventories, Sentinel-1/2, PlanetScope (Education & Research / NICFI where eligible), FABDEM/GLO-30, IMERG, SMAP, DHM gauges, NLCMS land cover, VIDA buildings, WorldPop. *New:* a **National Nepal Landslide Inventory (NNLI)** combining satellite-mapped scars with text-mined fatal-landslide records, with explicit completeness metadata per district — addressing exactly the deficiency Kincey et al. document.

**Input.** Rainfall (gauge-corrected), soil moisture, terrain, land cover, susceptibility, post-seismic legacy → nowcast. Imagery pairs → detection.
**Output.** Ward-level daily landslide probability with a plain-language advisory tier; and a national landslide polygon inventory with per-district completeness scores.

**Evaluation metrics.** Multitemporal TPR/FAR (matching LHASA v2's own hardest protocol so the comparison is honest), Brier score and reliability diagrams, precision-recall on detection, IoU on segmentation, **label-efficiency curves (performance vs number of local labels)**, and a district-stratified breakdown so that performance in under-mapped districts is not hidden by performance in the well-mapped Gorkha zone.

**Baselines.** LHASA v2 as-is over Nepal; a simple rainfall intensity-duration threshold (Guzzetti-class); from-scratch U-Net; ResU-Net (the Landslide4Sense winner).

**Novelty.** First Nepal validation and recalibration of a global landslide nowcast; first label-efficiency study of geospatial foundation models on Nepali landslides; first national inventory combining EO and Nepali-language text mining with explicit completeness metadata.

**Deployment scenario.** A daily advisory feed into BIPAD and to district and palika officials during the monsoon, with a colour-coded tier and a plain-language action ("tonight, households in the marked wards should sleep at the community building"). **[I] The product is the advisory protocol, not the probability map.**

**Difficulty 7. Cost:** USD 40–70 k over 18 months (compute for foundation-model fine-tuning is the main non-salary cost; PlanetScope access may be free under education programmes but should be budgeted). **Timeline:** 12 months to a strong paper, 24 to an operational advisory pilot in 2–3 districts. **Publication potential: high** — *NHESS*, *Landslides*, *Remote Sensing of Environment*, IGARSS.

**Risks [I].** Ground truth scarcity is the binding constraint and it is severe; the project may end up spending most of its effort building the inventory, which is fine but should be planned for. Advisory-tier products face the false-alarm/cry-wolf problem acutely because landslide nowcasts will fire on many nights.

## 12.4 Project SADAK — Post-Disaster Road Accessibility and Rescue-Priority Graph

*(सडक — "road".)*

**Research problem.** In 2015, 2023, 2024 and 2026 alike, blocked highways and destroyed bridges delayed both rescue arrival and relief delivery, and **in no event was a live road-passability map used operationally** [E/I]. In September 2024 the dominant death mechanism was people in vehicles trapped or swept on flooded and landslide-blocked highways at night [E]. In August 2026, 35 motorable and 45 suspension bridges and ~40 km of road were destroyed [E]. The published GNN routing work assumes uniform flood-driven speed reduction on dense urban grids; Nepal's failure mode is a **single landslide severing the only corridor** — a discrete, topologically different disruption that the existing methods do not model [E/I].

**Hypothesis [H].** (H1) Fusing CV-detected blockages from VHR/UAV imagery, SAR-derived flood and landslide extents, and NLP-extracted blockage reports into probabilistic edge removals on the OSM/HOT road graph produces reachability and travel-time estimates materially more accurate than any single source, validated against post-event ground truth from Nepal Army/police convoy logs and HOT mapping. (H2) In a mountain network, the *identity of the critical edges* is stable and computable in advance, so a pre-computed criticality ranking of Nepal's road segments — "which 200 segments, if cut, isolate the most people from the nearest hospital" — is a high-value static product that requires no real-time data at all.

**Research questions.** How accurately can road blockage be detected from each of PlanetScope, VHR and UAV imagery in Nepali terrain, and what is the latency of each? How do CV-derived and text-derived blockage evidence combine, and how often do they disagree? What is the isolation-index distribution across Nepal's 753 local units under simulated monsoon blockage scenarios? Does a GNN outperform straightforward probabilistic shortest-path computation at Nepal's graph scale, or is the simpler method sufficient — and if so, is that not the better operational answer?

**Target disasters.** All road-affecting hazards: landslides, floods, earthquakes, debris flows.

**AI methods.** Segmentation/detection CNNs or foundation-model fine-tunes for road-blockage and bridge-loss detection; SAR change detection for all-weather extent; graph neural networks and probabilistic shortest-path/percolation analysis on the road graph; multi-criteria ranking for rescue prioritisation. **[I] Include the simple baseline honestly — if Dijkstra over a probabilistically-thinned graph matches the GNN at Nepal's scale, say so; that is a useful result and a more deployable system.**

**NLP component.** Extract blockage, bridge-loss and passability statements from NDRRMA/police situation reports, Department of Roads notices, news and social media (Nepali and English), with the §8.3 schema and NepGeo geocoding, into `Road -[BLOCKED_BY]-> Hazard` edges with provenance.

**CV component.** The detection half.

**Datasets.** *Existing:* OSM/HOT Nepal road network (actively maintained, with a live Nepal Floods 2026 campaign), VIDA buildings, WorldPop, health-facility locations from HDX, Copernicus EMS EMSR927 damage products, Maxar/Vantor and Planet crisis imagery for Aug 2026, Sentinel-1. *New:* a **Nepal Road Blockage Dataset** — annotated blockage segments for the 2024 and 2026 events from VHR imagery plus text-extracted reports, with ground-truth reopening dates where obtainable from the Department of Roads. **[I] No such dataset exists anywhere; DeepGlobe and SpaceNet do not cover Nepal.**

**Input.** Pre/post imagery, SAR extent, situation-report text, road graph, exposure layers.
**Output.** (a) A live passability layer with per-edge confidence and provenance; (b) reachability and travel-time from each district HQ and hospital to each ward; (c) an isolation index and rescue-priority ranking; (d) a static national road-criticality ranking.

**Evaluation metrics.** Blockage detection precision/recall/F1 and latency by sensor; reachability accuracy against convoy/ground-truth logs; travel-time MAE; isolation-index validation against observed relief-arrival times; and a decision-relevant metric — **the rank correlation between the system's rescue priority and the priority responders retrospectively judge correct**.

**Baselines.** OSM graph with no damage information (current practice); manual situation-report reading; imagery-only; text-only.

**Novelty.** First road-passability dataset and accessibility model for Nepal; first treatment of single-corridor severance rather than urban flood degradation; first fusion of CV and Nepali-language NLP blockage evidence.

**Deployment scenario.** A layer in BIPAD and a mobile view for district EOCs, Nepal Army/APF logistics, and the Logistics Cluster. The static criticality ranking is deliverable to the Department of Roads immediately and requires no real-time system at all — **[I] which makes it an excellent early credibility-building deliverable.**

**Difficulty 7. Cost:** USD 40–65 k over 18 months. **Timeline:** static criticality product in 4–6 months; dataset and detection paper at 12–15 months; live layer at 18–24. **Publication potential: high** — *International Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction*, *Transportation Research Part C/D*, IEEE TGRS for the detection component.

**Risks [I].** Ground truth on road reopening is held by the Department of Roads and may be hard to obtain. VHR imagery is free only on Charter/Open Data activation, i.e. only for major events. The GNN may add nothing over simpler methods.

## 12.5 Project BIJULI — Lightning Nowcasting and Community Alerting

*(बिजुली — "lightning".)*

**Research problem.** Lightning kills **~68 people per year in NDRRMA's record and ~103 per year in the peer-reviewed analysis (930 deaths and 2,454 injuries over 2011–2020)**, concentrated in Okhaldhunga, Solukhumbu and other hill districts, and there is **no early-warning or forecasting system in Nepal at all** — a gap the study's authors state explicitly [E]. Detection networks (GLD360) already observe every strike; the missing element is nowcasting and dissemination, not sensing.

**Hypothesis [H].** A convective-cell nowcasting model using geostationary IR, lightning-detection-network history and terrain covariates can issue district- or cell-scale 30–60 minute lightning alerts in Nepal with skill sufficient to change outdoor-work and school behaviour, and a dissemination design targeted at farm workers, school authorities and FM radio can convert a meaningful share of that skill into avoided deaths.

**Research questions.** What nowcast skill (POD/FAR/CSI at 15/30/60 min) is achievable over Nepal's terrain with freely available inputs? Does terrain conditioning improve skill, given the strong orographic control on Himalayan convection? What alert threshold maximises expected lives saved under a cost-loss model that accounts for alert fatigue? What dissemination channel actually reaches an outdoor agricultural worker in Okhaldhunga at 15:00?

**Target disaster.** Lightning; secondarily hailstorm and windstorm, which share the convective driver.

**AI methods.** ConvLSTM / U-Net / diffusion-based nowcasting over satellite IR and lightning-density fields; gradient boosting for a district-day probability baseline; explicit calibration and cost-loss threshold selection.

**NLP component.** Minor — automated multilingual alert text generation and a light misinformation/rumour check on the alert channel.
**CV component.** The nowcasting model operates on gridded imagery, so it is CV in the sequence-prediction sense.

**Datasets.** *Existing:* GLD360 or equivalent lightning-detection data (**commercial — licensing is the main cost and the main risk**), geostationary IR (INSAR-3D/Himawari/FY-4 as available over Nepal), IMERG, ERA5, DHM stations, the published Nepal lightning fatality dataset (2011–2020, district-level), NDRRMA incident records. *New:* a Nepal lightning-fatality-and-exposure dataset extended past 2020 and geocoded to ward level, joined with the alert log for prospective evaluation.

**Input.** Satellite IR sequences, lightning density history, terrain, time of day/season.
**Output.** 15/30/60-minute cell-scale lightning probability → district and ward alerts via SMS, FM radio and school/agricultural-cooperative channels.

**Evaluation metrics.** POD, FAR, CSI, Brier score and reliability by lead time; **prospective behavioural evaluation** in pilot districts (did schools and cooperatives change behaviour?); and — the only metric that ultimately matters — a before/after fatality comparison in pilot versus control districts, acknowledging that the sample size makes statistical significance unlikely within the project's life.

**Baselines.** Climatological district-season lightning probability; persistence; a simple IR-threshold convective indicator.

**Novelty.** Low-to-moderate scientifically — lightning nowcasting is a mature field. **[I] The novelty is Nepal-specific: terrain-conditioned nowcasting in complex Himalayan orography, and a rigorous cost-loss dissemination design for a low-literacy, low-connectivity rural audience. The contribution is operational, not methodological, and the project should be honest about that.**

**Expected contribution.** The first lightning warning capability in a country where lightning is a top-four killer.

**Deployment scenario.** DHM issues; NDRRMA/BIPAD relays; district FM stations and SMS carry the last mile; schools and agricultural cooperatives are the primary organised recipients.

**Difficulty 5. Cost:** USD 30–55 k over 12–18 months, **dominated by lightning-detection data licensing**, which should be scoped before commitment (a partnership with the data provider on humanitarian terms is worth pursuing). **Timeline:** 9–12 months to a working nowcast; 18 to a district pilot. **Publication potential: moderate** — *Weather and Forecasting*, *Weather, Climate and Society*, *Natural Hazards*.

**Risks [I].** Data licensing may be prohibitive. Behavioural response is the real bottleneck and is outside the team's control. Alert fatigue in a high-frequency hazard is a serious design challenge — Nepal averages roughly 280 lightning incidents per year in NDRRMA's record, so a poorly-tuned system would be alerting constantly.

## 13.4 Which to start first, and why

**Start GHADI and NEPAL-CRISIS-IE together.** [I]

The argument for GHADI is that it is the only project on the list that attacks the mechanism that just killed a thousand people, it is the only one occupying genuine international white space, its primary input (FDSN seismic waveforms) is free and already flowing, and the political and funding window in Nepal for exactly this capability is open right now and will not stay open. Its weakness is execution risk: sparse training events, a hard signal-processing problem, and dependence on DHM cooperation.

The argument for pairing it with NEPAL-CRISIS-IE is risk management. NEPAL-CRISIS-IE's floor is high — a well-built low-resource dataset publishes regardless of how the models perform, and there is literally no prior Nepali work to be scooped by. It also produces the gazetteer (NepGeo) that HIMAL-SLIDE and SADAK both depend on, so it is the natural infrastructure project. And its missing-person application addresses a live, visible national need this month.

**Do not start three.** A team small enough to have written this brief is small enough that a third concurrent project would starve the first two. HIMAL-SLIDE should begin at month 9–12, once NepGeo exists. SADAK's *static* road-criticality product can be produced as a two-month side deliverable early (it needs only OSM, WorldPop and health-facility locations) to build institutional credibility, with the full project deferred. BIJULI should be pitched to a government or NGO partner as an operational programme rather than run as research — its value is deployment, not discovery.

| **Best for startup / commercialisation** | SADAK | Road passability and logistics routing have commercial buyers (insurance, telecoms, logistics, hydropower operators) beyond government; GHADI and BIJULI are public goods that should not be commercialised |

| **Best combining NLP + CV** | SADAK | Genuinely requires both — CV cannot see a blockage under cloud, text cannot geolocate precisely, and each corroborates the other. NEPAL-CRISIS-IE is a close second |
| **Best master's thesis** | NEPAL-CRISIS-IE, scoped to one channel | E.g. "Romanised Nepali disaster information extraction: a corpus and a transliteration-cascade evaluation." Bounded, publishable, needs no field deployment |
| **Best PhD / research lab programme** | GHADI | Four years of genuine science: signal discrimination on sparse networks, multi-modal fusion under cost-loss, graceful degradation, and an end-to-end lead-time analysis with real institutional deployment |

# 14. Dataset Architecture for the Top Projects

## 14.1 The five-layer model

Every dataset in this programme uses the same layering, so that products compose rather than fork:

| Layer | Content | Storage | Governance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L1 — Raw** | Immutable, as-acquired: waveforms (miniSEED), gauge series, satellite scenes (COG), UAV frames, raw text, PDFs of reports | Object store, content-addressed, write-once | Never edited. Provenance record per object |
| **L2 — Processed** | Georeferenced, normalised, resampled, cloud/terrain-masked, deduplicated, transliterated, tokenised | Parquet / Zarr / COG + STAC | Reproducible from L1 by a versioned pipeline |
| **L3 — Labels** | Human and machine annotations, each with annotator ID, timestamp, guideline version, and confidence | Separate store, joined by stable IDs | **Labels never overwrite L2.** Multiple label sets can coexist |
| **L4 — Multimodal associations** | Event-centric links: which waveform window, which gauge series, which scene, which text report, which polygon belong to the same physical event | Graph store (the §11.2 knowledge graph) | Association is itself an annotated, auditable claim |
| **L5 — Splits** | Train / validation / test with **explicit split policy** | Manifest files, versioned | See §14.4 — this is where most disaster-ML papers go wrong |

## 14.3 Project-specific dataset architectures

### 14.3.1 GHADI

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|
| L1 | Continuous miniSEED from NK network + regional FDSN stations (open); DHM gauge series at native sampling **[P]**; Sentinel-1/2 and Landsat archive over source zones; USGS catalogue |
| L2 | Instrument-response-removed, band-filtered, resampled waveform windows; spectrograms; gauge series gap-filled with explicit gap flags; per-station noise-climatology baselines by season and hour |

| L4 | `SeismicEvent —[SAME_PHYSICAL_EVENT]→ GaugeAnomaly —[SAME_PHYSICAL_EVENT]→ SatelliteObservation`, with the association itself carrying a confidence |
| L5 | **Held out by event, and by station-network configuration** — never random windows (§14.4) |

**Realism check.** Waveforms: fully open today. Gauges: require a DHM agreement, and this is the project's single biggest data dependency — but a fallback exists, since the *seismic-only* detector can be developed and published without gauge data, with gauge fusion added when access is granted. Satellite: open. **[I] Design the project so that a DHM refusal delays fusion but does not kill the project.**

### 14.3.2 NEPAL-CRISIS-IE

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|
| L1 | Raw messages with source, timestamp, platform/channel, and consent/licence status; audio for the ASR subset |

**Target composition (25,000–40,000 messages):** ~40% Devanagari native, ~30% romanised, ~20% code-mixed, ~10% ASR-transcribed; across at least four events; balanced so that no single event exceeds 40% of the corpus. **[I] The channel stratification is the scientific contribution — an unstratified Nepali corpus would be much easier to build and much less useful.**

|---|---|

## 14.4 Split policy — the part most disaster-ML papers get wrong

**[I] Random train/test splits on geospatial and event data produce inflated, non-reproducible results.** Three rules for this programme, and they should be stated in every paper:

1. **Spatial block holdout.** Hold out contiguous blocks (basins, or grid blocks larger than the spatial autocorrelation range), never random pixels or patches. Adjacent Sentinel-2 patches from the same landslide are not independent samples.
2. **Event holdout.** For anything event-driven — seismic detection, crisis text, damage assessment — hold out *entire events*. A model that has seen half the 2026 Bhote Koshi messages will trivially classify the other half.
3. **Temporal holdout for anything that will be deployed forward in time.** Train on the past, test on the future. Report both the spatial-block and the temporal result; if they diverge sharply, say so — that divergence is the honest estimate of deployment performance.

Additionally: **report a "geographic transfer" result** — train on well-mapped districts, test on under-mapped ones — because that is the actual deployment condition in Nepal, where 40% of the country has been mapped only once.

## 15.2 Annotation strategy

### Image labels
Flood / no-flood; landslide scar / deposit / no landslide; building damaged / destroyed / no visible damage / unknown-obscured; road blocked / passable / unknown; debris; fire; snow-ice; **and an explicit `unobservable` class for terrain shadow, layover and cloud**.

**[I] The `unknown-obscured` and `unobservable` classes are not bookkeeping — they are the mechanism that stops the model learning to guess in exactly the conditions where Nepal's imagery fails.** A dataset that forces annotators to choose "no damage" over a cloud is a dataset that teaches the model to say "no damage" when it cannot see.

### NLP labels
Location (with gazetteer ID, admin level, granularity flag); hazard type; casualty (dead/injured/missing, value + unit + certainty); missing person; infrastructure damage (type, status, severity); urgent request; shelter request; road blockage; severity; urgency; source reliability; **and explicit `not_stated` for every slot**.

### Quality protocol
1. Written guidelines, versioned, with worked Nepali examples for every ambiguous case.
2. A 200-item calibration round before production annotation; annotators who fall below threshold are retrained, not dropped silently.
3. **Double annotation on 20% throughout** (not just at the start), with adjudication by a senior annotator.
4. Report Cohen's κ for spans, Krippendorff's α for structured slots, **separately for Devanagari and romanised items**.
5. Adversarial review: a second pass specifically hunting for annotations that a model could get right for the wrong reason.
6. **Annotators are paid, credited, and their disagreements are published as part of the dataset** — disagreement on "is this severity high?" is signal about task difficulty, not noise to be discarded.

## 15.3 Handling Nepal's language reality

| Problem | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Nepali (Devanagari)** | NepBERTa/MuRIL encoders; note that byte-level BPE splits Devanagari at vowel diacritics, inflating token counts roughly 3× (~4.78 vs 1.58 tokens/word) — **a tokenizer-level defect that more pretraining does not fix**, so budget context length and cost accordingly, and consider a custom tokenizer |
| **English** | Standard; present in official reports, international media, and expat/diaspora posts |
| **Code-mixed Nepali-English** | ~33% of Nepali social comments are distinctly mixed in the one measured study; do not filter these out — they are the majority channel in urban Nepal |
| **Romanised Nepali** | **Store as a first-class channel, never normalise it away.** Evaluate both direct extraction and the transliteration cascade. MuRIL's transliterated pretraining is the most promising off-the-shelf starting point |
| **Spelling variation / no fixed orthography** | Character-level or subword-robust models; a normalisation lexicon built during annotation; never a hard spelling-correction step that silently destroys evidence |
| **Noisy social media** | Near-duplicate clustering before annotation, so annotators do not label the same forwarded message 40 times |
| **Duplicate reports** | Cluster by (space, time, content) similarity; **retain cluster size as a feature**, since repetition is weak evidence of salience but not of truth |
| **Misinformation** | Label `source_reliability` rather than `true/false`. **[I] A research team should not be adjudicating truth during a live disaster; it should be surfacing corroboration and letting authorities adjudicate** |
| **Place names** | The NepGeo gazetteer with Devanagari / romanised / English / pre-2015 VDC aliases for all 753 local units; resolution returns an admin level and a granularity warning, never a false point |

## 16.2 Technical specification

**Data schemas and metadata.** STAC for all raster assets, with a Nepal profile adding `terrain_observability`, `monsoon_flag` and `hazard_event_id`. FDSN StationXML for seismic metadata. The §14.2 event record as the canonical interchange format, versioned with a stated compatibility policy. **CAP 1.2 as the alert output format** — this is not optional; DHM ran its first national CAP training with WMO in April 2025 and is drafting SOPs, which is a narrow window in which a system that emits CAP natively becomes trivially interoperable instead of needing a retrofit [E].

**Geospatial indexing.** H3 at resolution 7–9 (roughly 5 km² to 0.1 km² cells) for exposure joins and spatial partitioning; administrative codes (province/district/palika/ward) as the primary human-facing key; explicit alias tables for the ~3,900 pre-2015 VDC names.

**Streaming vs batch.** Streaming for anything where latency is the product: seismic (SeedLink, continuous), gauge telemetry, text ingest, and alert feeds. Batch for imagery, reanalysis, susceptibility and model retraining. **[I] Keep the detection path — seismic and gauge — physically and operationally separate from the analysis path. The detection path must survive when the analysis path is down, because in a disaster the analysis path will be down.**

**Model serving.** Detection models run as small, always-on services with a hard latency budget (target: inference in <5 s on a 240 s waveform window, on CPU). Heavy CV models run as batch jobs. Every model is versioned, and every alert records the exact model version that produced it.

**MLOps.** Data versioning (DVC or LakeFS), experiment tracking, containerised reproducible pipelines, automated regression tests on a frozen benchmark set, canary deployment with shadow-mode evaluation before any model goes live. **A model may not be promoted to live alerting without a completed shadow period of at least one full monsoon.**

**Monitoring.** Input drift (station availability, cloud fraction, message volume by channel), output drift (alert rate vs climatology), latency budget per stage, and **sensor mortality tracking** — the 2026 event proved gauges die mid-event, and the system must notice and re-plan rather than silently lose a channel.

**Uncertainty.** Every probability calibrated and reported with an interval; every location with a radius; every time with a window. **A single scalar with no uncertainty must not appear anywhere in an operational output.**

**Human-in-the-loop verification.** The gate shows the operator the *evidence* — the waveform, the gauge trace, the imagery, the corroborating reports — not a number. Operator decisions are logged and become training data for threshold refinement.

**Audit logs.** Append-only, tamper-evident, recording input snapshot hash, model version, score, gate decision, operator identity, dissemination result, and downstream acknowledgements. **[I] This is the mechanism that makes accountability possible after a false alarm or a missed event, and given the DRRM Regulations' language about not causing "unnecessary panic", it is the artefact that protects both the operator and the system.**

**Fail-safe.** Degrade to the surviving modality, and say so in the alert ("seismic detection only; no downstream corroboration available — 3 of 5 gauges offline"). If the fusion layer fails, individual detectors still emit. If the network fails, a local edge device at a district EOC retains the last known state and can trigger a local siren. **Never fail silent.**

**Offline / low-bandwidth operation.** District EOC dashboards must work on intermittent 3G with local caching and delta sync. Alerts must degrade to plain SMS text and to a two-tone siren pattern. Mobile clients must function fully offline for map and contact data. **[I] This is not a nice-to-have in a country where the disaster itself destroys the backhaul — in August 2026 the flood destroyed 80 bridges and 40 km of road along the same corridors the fibre follows.**

**Privacy and security.** Personal data (hotline transcripts, missing-person records, any mobility data) is stored separately from hazard data, encrypted at rest, access-controlled by role, with every access logged. Public APIs expose hazard and aggregate exposure only. See §19.

**Disaster-resilient infrastructure.** Primary hosting on government infrastructure (Government Integrated Data Centre) with an out-of-country replica; the detection path additionally deployable as a self-contained appliance at DHM and at district EOCs so that it survives the loss of Kathmandu connectivity. Power: UPS plus solar for edge nodes, given Nepal's supply reliability.

| **T1 — Advisory to officials** | Alert reaches DHM/NDRRMA/district EOC staff only | Human sends onward | After one monsoon of shadow with acceptable FAR |
| **T2 — Human-approved public alert** | Alert drafted, CAP formed, dissemination pre-staged; a human presses send | Duty officer | After documented T1 performance |
| **T3 — Auto-fire, top tier only** | Highest-confidence corroborated detections fire automatically; humans can cancel within a defined window | Post-hoc review | Only with explicit NDRRMA authorisation and a legal basis |

**[I] Most disaster-AI projects fail by attempting T2 on day one. The tiering is the adoption strategy, not a technical detail.**

# 17. Evaluation Framework

## 17.1 The four questions every model must answer

Benchmark accuracy is not evidence of usefulness. Every model in this programme is evaluated against four questions, in order:

1. **Is it right?** — standard ML metrics, on honest splits (§14.4).
2. **Is it right *here*?** — performance under Nepal's constraints: monsoon cloud, steep-terrain SAR distortion, sparse networks, degraded sensors, romanised script, out-of-region transfer.
3. **Does it help someone decide?** — decision-relevant metrics: lead time delivered, isolation index accuracy, rank correlation with expert priority, null-precision.
4. **What happens when it is wrong?** — false-alarm cost, missed-event cost, failure mode characterisation, and graceful degradation.

## 17.2 Metric specification by task

| Task | Primary metrics | Nepal-specific stress tests | Decision metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cascade detection (GHADI) | POD, FAR per station-month, ROC/AUC, **latency distribution** | N-station dropout; monsoon noise season; co-seismic coda | **Lead time delivered at named settlements** |
| Gauge anomaly | Precision/recall, time-to-detect | Sensor loss mid-event; telemetry gaps | Minutes of warning added over stage threshold |
| Landslide nowcast | Multitemporal TPR/FAR (LHASA's hardest protocol), Brier, reliability | Under-mapped district transfer; IMERG bias | Advisory nights per season vs events captured |
| Landslide detection (CV) | IoU, precision/recall by size bin, **label-efficiency curve** | Cloud-masked evaluation; small (<50 m) slide bin reported separately | Fraction of *fatal* landslides detected |
| Flood mapping (SAR) | IoU, F1 vs EMS reference polygons | **Reported separately for Terai vs mountain valley**; observability-masked | Extent error where population is exposed |
| Building damage | Per-class F1, especially the rare severe class; ordinal error | OOD test on a different event/district (xFBD protocol) | Triage rank correlation with field assessment |
| Road blockage | Precision/recall, latency by sensor | Cloud; night; narrow gorge | Reachability accuracy vs convoy logs |
| NLP IE | Per-type F1 (macro **and** micro), slot exact match, numeric tolerance accuracy, **palika-level geocoding accuracy**, **null-precision** | **Stratified by script and channel**; adversarial spelling variants | Actionable-report score; time to triage a report backlog |
| Record linkage | Pairwise P/R/F1, cluster purity | Name variants across scripts; foreign names | Duplicate reduction and correct-match rate under human adjudication |
| Sitrep generation | Faithfulness to source records, **hallucination rate**, human preference | Nepali output quality; numeric fidelity | Analyst time saved with no factual regressions |

## 17.3 The cost-loss framework

Every alerting system in this programme must state its operating point explicitly, as a function of the cost ratio:

> **C_miss / C_false_alarm**

For a cascade surge in an inhabited valley, the ratio is enormous — a missed event costs lives, a false alarm costs an evacuation and some trust. For a landslide advisory that fires 25 nights a season, the ratio is far smaller, because alert fatigue is itself a mortality risk. **[I] Publishing the assumed ratio, and the sensitivity of the recommended threshold to it, is a requirement of this programme and is largely absent from the disaster-ML literature.**

## 17.4 Validation against operational reality

- **Retrospective event replay.** Every system is replayed against the documented events of §3, with only the data that existed at the time, and reports what it would have produced and when. **[I] For GHADI, "how many minutes would this have bought on 26 August 2026?" is the headline result of the entire project.**
- **Shadow operation.** A full monsoon of live shadow running before any public alerting.
- **Operator studies.** Can a district officer read the output and decide in under two minutes? Test it with actual district officers, not researchers.
- **Post-event evaluation.** Every real event during the project becomes an evaluation case, with a documented, pre-registered analysis protocol so the evaluation is not written after the outcome is known.

## 17.5 What would falsify each project's core hypothesis

| Project | Falsifying result |
|---|---|
| GHADI | Mass-movement signals cannot be discriminated from noise on Nepal's sparse network at any useful false-alarm rate; or detection latency exceeds surge travel time for the settlements at risk |
| NEPAL-CRISIS-IE | Annotation of romanised/code-mixed disaster text cannot reach κ ≥ 0.7, making a reliable gold standard impossible; or gazetteer-grounded geoparsing does not beat LLM zero-shot |
| HIMAL-SLIDE | Nepal recalibration does not improve on LHASA v2's multitemporal TPR; or foundation-model fine-tuning offers no label-efficiency advantage over from-scratch CNNs on Nepali landslides |
| SADAK | Fused blockage evidence is no more accurate than reading situation reports; or the accessibility model's priority ranking does not correlate with expert judgement |
| BIJULI | Nowcast skill over Nepal's terrain is no better than climatology at 30–60 min; or alert fatigue destroys behavioural response in the pilot |

**[I] Stating these in advance is the difference between a research programme and an advocacy exercise. Each of these outcomes is publishable.**

# 18. Government and Field Deployment Strategy

## 18.1 The institutional map, and where an AI output must enter

Nepal's architecture is set by the **Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act, 2074 (2017)** and the **DRRM Regulations, 2076 (2019)**, three-tiered to match federalisation (7 provinces, 77 districts, 753 local units) [E].

| Body | Role | Relevance to an AI system |
|---|---|---|
| **National Council for DRRM** (chaired by the Prime Minister) | Policy; meets ≥2×/year | Not operational, but sets the mandate NDRRMA is judged against (National DRR Policy 2018; DRR Strategic Action Plan 2018–2030; Sendai reporting) |
| **Executive Committee** (Home Minister) | Approves integrated plans; directs Army/Police/APF | The authority that can mandate use of a system |
| **NDRRMA** | Operational secretariat; owns the **national disaster database (BIPAD)**, the national EWS mandate, and capacity support to lower tiers | **The single most plausible institutional home.** It already owns the closest thing Nepal has to a common operating picture, and BIPAD is cited regionally (including by Bangladesh) as a model |
| **MoHA** | Coordination and security chain; CDO notifications still route through it | **A documented ownership ambiguity: sources disagree whether NEOC now sits under NDRRMA or MoHA.** Resolve with both, do not assume |
| **DHM** (under Energy/Water Resources, *not* Home Affairs) | Sole technical authority for weather and flood forecasts; River Watch; colour-coded flood status; ran Nepal's **first national CAP training with WMO in April 2025** and is drafting CAP SOPs | **The concrete near-term integration point for a machine-readable alert feed** |
| **Department of Mines and Geology / NEMRC** | Geohazard and seismic monitoring; operates the NK network | Data partner for GHADI |
| **Provincial DRRM committees** | Level-2 disasters; rapid impact assessment; recommend emergency status | Secondary users |
| **District DDMCs (chaired by the CDO)** | Historical warning-relay node; coordinate Police, APF and Red Cross | **The tier that must be able to act on an output in under two minutes** |
| **753 local governments (palikas)** | Legally own Level-0/1 disasters, must hold the line on Level-2/3, maintain LDCRPs, local emergency funds, vulnerable-population databases and building-code enforcement | **The sharpest capacity gap: legally the first responder and final messenger, with the least ability to interpret a probabilistic output** |
| **Nepal Police, APF, Nepal Army** | SAR and evacuation. In Aug 2026: ~21,000 security personnel, 7,912 police, ~2,000 Army with 7 Army helicopters **plus 8 privately chartered tourism helicopters** | Nepal's air SAR capacity is a mixed military/commercial fleet mobilised ad hoc; **no INSARAG-classified domestic team was identified** — Nepal is a recipient of classified international teams |
| **Nepal Red Cross Society** | Activates its own Early Action Protocols in parallel with government | Named CAP partner; a fast-moving adoption channel |
| **UN OCHA / HCT / clusters** | Engage on request for international assistance; OCHA runs the largest anticipatory-action framework by budget (USD 20.5 m) | Funding and legitimacy channel |

## 18.2 The realistic end-to-end workflow

```
T+0:00   HAZARD SIGNAL
         Satellite/GLOFAS forecast, an upstream community gauge reader, or DHM's
         hydrological and meteorological stations register a threshold crossing.
         For cross-border cryospheric hazard the signal may originate inside
         Tibet with NO data-sharing channel — a structural blind spot.

T+0:01   [PROPOSED] DETECTION LAYER (GHADI)
         Seismic classifier flags a non-tectonic mass-movement signal;
         gauge rate-of-rise anomaly corroborates. Fusion emits a calibrated
         score and a draft CAP message.          ← THIS STAGE DOES NOT EXIST TODAY

T+0:02–1:00  HUMAN VERIFICATION
         DHM duty forecaster reviews the evidence and, per the emerging CAP SOP,
         approves or holds. Note: DHM's flood section runs on roughly five
         professional staff plus six technicians.

T+1:00–3:00  AUTHORITY / TRIGGER
         DHM bulletin issued. Separately, if the event matches an anticipatory-
         action framework, a DUAL-CONDITION TRIGGER (government bulletin +
         GLOFAS 3-day 70% probability, OR real-time danger level — Karnali at
         Chisapani 10.5 m / Koshi at Chatara 7.0 m) releases pre-agreed
         WFP/UNICEF cash and NRCS action with no fresh human decision.
         ← THE ONE PART OF THE SYSTEM ALREADY BUILT FOR AUTOMATED TRIGGERING

T+3:00–6:00  DISSEMINATION
         CDO/DDMC relays via SMS to officials; FM radio (still dominant in rural
         Nepal); electronic display boards with automatic sirens at gauge sites;
         Police/NRCS cascade to community volunteers; and — in CBEWS basins —
         upstream gauge readers phoning downstream contacts directly, a parallel,
         faster, more fragile channel.

T+2:00–8:00  COMMUNITY ACTION  ← THE BINDING CONSTRAINT
         Terai/Karnali CBEWS deliver a documented 2–3 hours of lead time under
         deterministic methods, extendable to 7–8 hours where probabilistic

         For a dam-break surge, lead time can be minutes or negative.

T+6:00+  RESPONSE
         Army/APF/Police SAR; chartered helicopters; NRCS and cluster relief.
         Infrastructure damage frequently severs the telecom backhaul that
```

## 18.4 The adoption strategy

**1. Build on BIPAD; never compete with it.** NDRRMA already operates BIPAD as the national disaster-information system. Any AI layer stands the best chance of survival as a **module feeding BIPAD's data layer and dashboard**, not a parallel platform. **[I] The classic donor-project death is a standalone tool that nobody logs into after the funding ends.**

**2. Emit CAP from day one, while the standard is still being written.** DHM ran its first national CAP training in April 2025 and is drafting SOPs [E]. A system specified to emit CAP 1.2 natively becomes trivially interoperable with whatever SOP DHM finalises. Coordinate directly with DHM and CARD, and flag the missing NTA/cell-broadcast link to donors as a gap worth funding — **that flag is itself a contribution.**

**3. Government co-ownership from day one, not "capacity building" at the end.** Embed NDRRMA and DHM staff as co-developers and co-owners of model outputs and thresholds, mirroring how the anticipatory-action frameworks already co-design triggers with WFP, OCHA and NRCS.

**4. Design for the capacity of the tier that must act.** Palikas are legally the first responders and the weakest link. The value proposition must be *simplifying* the CDO/palika decision — a colour-coded, CAP-formatted, plain-language trigger — not delivering richer probabilistic output that nobody can interpret in a two-hour window.

**5. Enter through anticipatory action, because it is already automated.** The OCHA/WFP Karnali–Koshi framework runs on hard dual-condition numeric triggers that release money without a fresh human decision [E]. **[I] This is the one place in the Nepali system already comfortable with automated triggering. A new model only has to outperform an accepted trigger — it does not have to build institutional trust from zero. This is the single most important adoption insight in this report.**

**6. Budget operations and maintenance with a named government budget code.** Given the documented pattern of donor-funded monitoring infrastructure not being restored after damage, reviewers at NDRRMA will ask. Have the answer.

**7. Deliver something useful in month 3.** The open-API integration of §6.2 (GDACS, FIRMS, LHASA v2, Google Flood Hub, GLOFAS piped into one BIPAD-compatible view) and SADAK's static road-criticality ranking are both achievable in weeks and both immediately useful. **[I] Institutional credibility is earned with early, unglamorous deliverables, and it is the currency that later buys access to DHM telemetry and BIPAD records.**

# 19. Ethical, Safety and Privacy Considerations

## 19.1 The false-alarm problem

An alerting system in Nepal faces an asymmetric but not one-sided cost. A missed cascade surge costs lives at the scale of hundreds. A false alarm costs an evacuation, economic disruption, and — cumulatively — the credibility that makes the next true alarm work. **[I] The honest position is that these must be traded explicitly (§17.3) and publicly, with the assumed cost ratio stated, rather than hidden inside a threshold.** For high-frequency hazards (landslide advisories, lightning) alert fatigue is a *mortality* risk, not merely an annoyance, and the design must treat it as such.

## 19.2 Accountability and legal exposure

Nepal's DRRM Regulations prohibit transmitting information that "may create unnecessary panic or fear" [E]. This creates real institutional risk-aversion toward automated probabilistic alerting. Two implications: (i) the **human-in-the-loop gate and the tiered deployment model of §16.3 are legal architecture, not just engineering**; (ii) **the append-only audit log is the artefact that protects the operator**, by making it demonstrable what the system said, when, on what evidence, and who decided what. The L'Aquila case — where scientists were convicted and later acquitted on appeal over seismic risk communication — is the standard reference for why this matters, in either direction: both under- and over-warning carry exposure.

**[I] A research team must be explicit that it is providing decision support, not making decisions, and must resist pressure to have the model "just issue the warning" before the legal basis exists.**

## 19.3 Privacy

| Data type | Risk | Required control |
|---|---|---|
| **Missing-person and casualty records** | Re-identification; distress; misuse for extortion or trafficking; wrongful notification of death | Government-hosted, non-public, role-based access, per-record audit log, human adjudication before any merge, formal data-sharing agreement, defined retention and deletion |
| **Hotline audio and transcripts** | Highly sensitive; callers have no expectation of research use | De-identification at ingest; explicit authorisation; never used for public demos; never in an open dataset |
| **Face imagery of victims** | Severe. Enables surveillance repurposing; dignity harm to the dead and their families | **Default: do not build face matching.** If ever built, only with explicit family opt-in, government operation, no retention beyond the event, and an independent ethics review |
| **Mobility / CDR** | Population-scale re-identification | Aggregation thresholds, differential-privacy noise, operator agreement, no individual-level analysis. The 2015 Ncell–Flowminder arrangement is the precedent and was strictly one-off [E] |
| **Social media** | Individuals did not consent to research use | Publish IDs and annotations rather than text where platform terms require; honour deletion; never re-publish identifiable distress content |
| **Household-level census / cadastre** | Re-identification in small wards | Use ward aggregates; unit-record microdata only under NSO authorisation, on their terms [E] |

Nepal's applicable framework includes the Individual Privacy Act 2018; a comprehensive data-protection statute could not be confirmed as enacted in this review and should be checked with counsel before any personal-data component begins [E — unverified].

## 19.4 Bias and equity

- **Geographic bias.** 40% of Nepal has been landslide-mapped once; models trained on the well-mapped Gorkha zone will underperform in Karnali and Sudurpashchim — **the districts with the least capacity to notice.** Mandatory: report district-stratified performance and publish a coverage/confidence map alongside every product.
- **Linguistic bias.** A system that works in Devanagari and fails in romanised Nepali silently serves urban, younger, more connected users better than everyone else. Stratified reporting (§8.4) is an equity control, not just a scientific one.
- **Connectivity bias.** Crowdsourced reporting systematically under-represents the hardest-hit remote communities — the documented lesson from Quakemap.org in 2015 [E]. Never let report *volume* drive resource allocation; weight by exposure, not by how loud a district is online.
- **Gender.** Nepali alert message design has been documented as not considering women as intended recipients [E]. Message design, channel choice and evaluation must include women explicitly.
- **Disability and literacy.** Audio and siren channels are not redundancy — they are the primary channel for a substantial population.

## 19.5 Cybersecurity and adversarial risk

A national alerting system is critical infrastructure. Threats: spoofed alerts causing mass panic; suppression of a real alert; poisoning of the crowdsourced report channel; and compromise of a personal-data store. Controls: signed CAP messages, authenticated dissemination paths, rate limits and provenance requirements on report ingestion, out-of-band verification for T3 auto-fire, and a documented incident-response plan. **[I] A disaster-alerting system is a uniquely attractive target precisely because it is trusted, and this receives almost no attention in the disaster-AI literature.**

## 19.6 Model failure during emergencies

The failure mode that matters is not degraded accuracy — it is **confident wrongness at the worst moment**. Requirements: graceful degradation with explicit statement of which channels are live; hard-coded sanity bounds (a physically impossible surge velocity or casualty count is rejected, not reported); a manual override that a duty officer can exercise in one action; and a rule that **no generative model ever produces a number** — numbers come from structured records, and language models only phrase them.

## 19.7 Dual use and research ethics

Population-exposure models, building-vulnerability layers and mobility analyses have obvious non-humanitarian uses. Publish methods and aggregate results; be deliberate about what granularity of exposure data is released publicly. Annotators must be paid fairly and warned about exposure to distressing content, with the right to withdraw. Community consent should be sought before any field UAV survey. And the team should be honest with communities that a warning system will sometimes be wrong.

# 21. Recommended Priority Project

## 21.1 The decision

**Build GHADI. Pair it with NEPAL-CRISIS-IE. Defer everything else.**

## 21.2 Why GHADI

**It attacks the mechanism that just killed a thousand people, and no existing system in the world addresses it.** [I] Every operational tool covering Nepal is rainfall- or stage-driven and was blind to a dry-day, cross-border, cryospheric cascade [E]. The initiating mass movement produced a seismic signal that USGS catalogued [E]. That signal was public, on open data, minutes before the water arrived. Nobody was listening. That is not a marginal improvement opportunity; it is an unoccupied capability.

**The counterfactual is unusually well evidenced for a disaster-AI proposal.** Two documented Nepali cases show that short-notice warning works in Nepali conditions with Nepali institutions: Thame in August 2024 (self-evacuation on sight, zero deaths, in a village that had no warning system at all) and Bidur in August 2026 (a phone call, ~14 minutes, 1,643 children evacuated) [E]. The surge took an estimated 20–40 minutes to reach the first settlements. A detection at T+2 minutes and dissemination at T+3 leaves 17–37 minutes. **[H] That is more than twice the notice Bidur actually received.**

**The primary input is free and already flowing.** NK network waveforms are FDSN-registered and reachable through standard clients today [E]. No new hardware, no procurement, no import clearance. The project can produce its headline retrospective result without a single new sensor.

**The training data problem is solvable, narrowly.** Large, well-recorded Himalayan cascade events are rare — but the 2015 Gorkha earthquake triggered 4,312+ mapped landslides with published satellite inventories [E], which is the only mass-movement population in the Himalaya large enough to train on. Exploiting it is itself a research contribution.

**It is publishable at the top.** No real-time seismic mass-movement detection exists in the Himalaya [E — absence]. The detection science, the multimodal corroboration-under-cost-loss framing, the graceful-degradation requirement, and the end-to-end lead-time replay of 26 August 2026 are four separable contributions.

**The political window is open now and will close.** Nepal has just filed its first claim to the UN Loss and Damage Fund, has USD 42 m+ pledged, and has a national conversation about early warning that did not exist in July [E]. **[I] Funding and institutional access for exactly this capability will never be easier to obtain than in the next twelve months.**

## 21.3 Why pair it with NEPAL-CRISIS-IE

GHADI's risk is real: sparse events, a hard signal problem, and a dependency on DHM. NEPAL-CRISIS-IE is the hedge. Its floor is high — a well-built low-resource corpus publishes regardless of model performance, and there is **zero** prior Nepali disaster-NLP work to be scooped by [E]. It produces NepGeo, which HIMAL-SLIDE and SADAK both need. And its missing-person application addresses a visible national need this month, which is worth more for institutional access than any amount of methodological elegance.

**[I] The pairing is the recommendation. One high-variance system project with an unclaimed prize; one low-variance infrastructure project that publishes and builds the shared assets. That is the correct risk posture for a small team with limited money and a twelve-month horizon.**

# 22. Open Research Gaps

Beyond the twelve gaps in §5.11, this review surfaced a further set that no project here claims, and that are offered to the wider community:

1. **Earthquake early warning feasibility for Nepal, honestly assessed.** Nobody has published a quantitative analysis of achievable warning time across Nepal's population distribution given near-source rupture geometry and a realistic station network. The answer might be "5 seconds for Kathmandu, 40 for Biratnagar" — and that answer would change national policy either way.
2. **A terrain-observability atlas for Nepal.** What fraction of Nepal's inhabited terrain is unobservable by Sentinel-1 due to layover and shadow, by pass direction and season? Nobody has published this, and every SAR product for Nepal implicitly depends on it.
3. **Cloud-free observation climatology for Nepal at sub-district resolution.** The 16.8-day Gorkha figure exists; a systematic national atlas does not.
4. **Nepali ASR on real emergency-call audio.** Every published Nepali WER comes from read or prompted speech. Nobody knows the real number, and it determines whether voice triage is possible at all.
5. **Alert fatigue in South Asian early-warning systems.** No quantitative evidence on cry-wolf effects in Nepal was found, yet every alerting threshold in this report depends on assumptions about it.
6. **The economics of anticipatory action under model uncertainty.** Nepal's AA frameworks use fixed thresholds. What is the optimal trigger under a calibrated probabilistic forecast with a stated cost-loss ratio, and how much money does the current fixed threshold leave on the table?
7. **Transboundary hazard data-sharing as a technical problem, not only a diplomatic one.** What can be inferred about upstream Tibetan conditions from open satellite and seismic data alone, and how much of the warning gap could be closed without any agreement with China? **[I] This is a genuinely valuable and politically tractable research question, and GHADI is its first instantiation.**
8. **Sensor mortality modelling.** Four of five gauges were destroyed on 26 August 2026. How should a network be designed, and how should inference be structured, when the sensors are inside the hazard?
9. **Does a knowledge graph measurably improve emergency decisions?** All the precedents are prototypes with no operational evaluation. A controlled study with real district officers would be a first.
10. **Nepali-language evaluation of frontier LLMs on structured extraction**, including null-precision — no published Nepali evaluation of this kind was found.
11. **The reliability of casualty statistics themselves.** Every major Nepali event shows 2–3× disagreement between sources for weeks. A methodological study of how to reconcile police, army, hospital and community reports — and how to report uncertainty in official statistics — would improve every downstream analysis, including this one.
12. **Post-disaster misinformation in Nepali**, which nobody has characterised, let alone modelled.
13. **Community trust and warning response in Nepal.** Thame self-evacuated; Bidur evacuated on a phone call; the October 2025 response was praised. What made those work? The mechanism is under-studied relative to its importance.

# Researcher's Decision Memo

**To:** the Nepal disaster-AI research team
**Date:** 2 September 2026
**Subject:** Where to focus first, and why

**1. The problem is not prediction.** Nepal's chronic killers — landslides (859 deaths / 6 years), lightning (408), floods (260) — have no warning system at all, and its catastrophic tail is now dominated by cryospheric cascades that carry no meteorological precursor. Seven days ago a dry-day ice-rock collapse inside Tibet killed over a thousand people in the Bhote Koshi valley. Every forecasting system covering Nepal was structurally blind to it. What saved 1,643 schoolchildren in Bidur was a phone call and 679,295 SMS messages fired in one minute. **The technology that saved them already exists in Nepal. The trigger that should have fired it twenty minutes earlier does not.**

**2. Build that trigger.** Project **GHADI**: detect large mass movements in Himalayan headwaters from continuous seismic waveforms within 60–180 seconds, corroborate with river-gauge rate-of-rise anomalies, and emit a CAP alert into the SMS and siren infrastructure Nepal already operates. The seismic data is free and already flowing through FDSN. The initiating event on 26 August produced a signal strong enough that USGS first catalogued it as an M4.4 earthquake. That signal was public, minutes before the water. Nobody was listening. **No such system exists anywhere in the Himalaya.** The surge took 20–40 minutes to reach the first settlements; a detection at T+2 minutes buys 17–37 minutes. That is more than twice what Bidur got.

**3. Pair it with a dataset project, because GHADI could fail.** Project **NEPAL-CRISIS-IE**: the first Nepali, romanised and code-mixed disaster information-extraction corpus, gazetteer, model and evaluation protocol — plus a missing-person record-linkage tool for the 4,606 people still unaccounted for. There is no Nepali disaster text corpus in existence. Nothing to be scooped by, a near-certain publication, and it produces the gazetteer that every other project on the list depends on.

**4. Defer the rest, but ship two things in month three.** HIMAL-SLIDE starts at month 9. SADAK's full build waits — but its *static* road-criticality ranking ("which 200 road segments, if cut, isolate the most people from a hospital") needs only OSM, WorldPop and health-facility locations and can be delivered in weeks. Alongside it, pipe GDACS, FIRMS, LHASA v2, GLOFAS and Google Flood Hub — all free, all open APIs, all already covering Nepal, none currently in any Nepali government dashboard — into one BIPAD-compatible view. Neither is research. Both are the currency that buys you DHM telemetry and BIPAD access.

**5. Three letters, week one.** NDRRMA (does BIPAD have an API?), DHM (can we have gauge series at native sampling, and where is the CAP SOP?), DMG/NEMRC (waveform access and collaboration). Half this programme is gated on those answers, and none of them is a research question.

**6. Enter through anticipatory action.** Nepal's OCHA/WFP/NRCS frameworks already release money automatically on hard numeric triggers — Chisapani at 10.5 m, Chatara at 7.0 m, GLOFAS at 70%. It is the one part of the Nepali system already comfortable with automated decisions. A new model there only has to beat an accepted threshold. Everywhere else, you have to build institutional trust from zero.

**7. What not to do.** Do not propose earthquake prediction; it is not possible. Do not build another rainfall-driven flood model for the gauged Terai; that space is occupied and the deaths are elsewhere. Do not build a parallel platform to BIPAD; it will be orphaned. Do not assume optical imagery during the monsoon; parts of northern Nepal have no cloud-free days between June and September. Do not point an off-the-shelf multimodal LLM at Nepali disaster imagery or text and believe the output; measure the hallucination rate first, and publish it.

**8. The one-sentence version.** *Stop trying to forecast the disaster and start trying to hear it start — then make sure the phone rings.*
